## Recall and substandard-medicine alerts never reach the dispenser or the user

*Id:* `medicine-recall-alerts-last-mile` · *Merged from:* Recalled, substandard and falsified medicine batches keep being dispensed because regulato; Drug and medical-device safety recalls never reach the people actually using the product

**Who.** Patients buying medicines from retail pharmacies, drug shops and patent-medicine vendors in LMICs, above all children (the 2022-2025 diethylene-glycol cough-syrup deaths were almost all under-fives in The Gambia, Indonesia, Uzbekistan and Madhya Pradesh, India). Dispensers and procurement staff in India (CDSCO publishes 50-170 'Not of Standard Quality' batches a month as PDFs), Nigeria (NAFDAC numbered alerts), Kenya (171 recalls 2016-2025 via website and social media), Rwanda, Ghana, Tanzania, Morocco (2025: recalled Ludiomil still on shelves because the alert was only posted on the agency website). Cross-border importers: Rwanda recalled a Kenyan-made product; a COMESA 2026 notice warned recalled batches had likely reached other member states. Hospital pharmacies and faith-based supply organisations (MEDS Kenya, JMS Uganda, CHAG Ghana) doing manual recall checks. In high-income countries: patients on recalled drugs (2018-2019 nitrosamine valsartan/losartan/irbesartan recalls touched tens of millions of prescriptions), home-device users (June 2021 Philips Respironics CPAP/ventilator recall, ~5.5 million US and ~15 million devices worldwide), implant recipients, family caregivers, and small independent pharmacies, home-health agencies, DME suppliers and clinics with no recall-management staff.

**Scale.** WHO (2017, still its current fact-sheet figure) estimates 1 in 10 medical products in LMICs is substandard or falsified (10.5% of ~48,000 samples, 88 countries) and that LMICs spend US$30.5 billion a year on them. Ozawa et al. (Am J Trop Med Hyg 2022; 130 studies, 95,520 samples) find 12.4% (95% CI 10.2-14.6%) of essential-medicine samples in LMICs fail at least one quality test, ~18.9% in Africa. Modelled (2017, high uncertainty): 72,000-169,000 child pneumonia deaths/yr from substandard antibiotics (Univ. of Edinburgh) and ~116,000 excess malaria deaths/yr in sub-Saharan Africa (LSHTM). Confirmed: >300 children died in 2022 from DEG/EG-contaminated syrups in The Gambia, Indonesia and Uzbekistan (WHO, Jan 2023); at least 20 under-fives died in Madhya Pradesh in Sept-Oct 2025 from Coldrif syrup with DEG ~500x the limit; WHO issued 9 cough-syrup warnings in 3 years, 5 for Indian-made products. WHO GSMS has logged >10,000 suspect products since 2013 but issued only 5 public alerts in 2024 and 7 in 2025. CDSCO flagged 48 NSQ batches in Aug 2024, 167 in Dec 2025, 111 in May 2026; India still has no binding recall law. US (unverified, to re-check): FDA Jan 2024 Philips update reported >116,000 device reports incl. 561 deaths; HHS OIG (2017) found Medicare paid ~$1.5bn 2005-2014 for seven recalled/failed cardiac devices; CDC 2023 artificial-tears outbreak: 81 infections, 4 deaths, 14 vision-loss cases. The share of recalled units actually recovered is measured nowhere.

**Why solutions fail.** (1) Regulators publish alerts as PDFs or web posts on their own sites, one jurisdiction at a time, with no structured batch-level data, no push channel to dispensers and no cross-border propagation (Morocco 2025; CDSCO monthly PDFs; Kenya PPB website/social media). (2) The recall system is legally built around consignees: the FDA enforcement schema records how the firm notified 'Consignee or Public' (letter, press release, e-mail, phone); there is no field or process for the end user, and effectiveness checks run down the distribution chain, not to patients. (3) Pharmacies record NDC but rarely lot numbers on patient profiles, and most LMIC pharmacies lack batch-level inventory at all, so lot-specific recalls cannot be matched to shelves or patients. (4) FDA device-recall records carry no UDI field while GUDID carries identifiers, so matching an implant card to a recall needs a crosswalk no public tool builds; openFDA's own device-recall pipeline fell back to scraping FDA HTML because the structured feed stopped. (5) Manufacturer authentication (mPedigree, Sproxil, GS1) only covers opted-in brands, verifies the pack not the contents, and says nothing about a genuine product later declared NSQ. (6) USP's Medicines Quality Database covers 2003-2013 in 17 countries; WHO GSMS reports are not public. (7) Existing aggregators (openFDA, DeepRecall) are US/HIC-centric; none covers NAFDAC, CDSCO, Ghana FDA or Kenya PPB. (8) Institutional services (ECRI Alerts Tracker, RASMAS) are priced for hospital systems; adherence apps are not lot-aware. (9) Public notification is broadcast, so individual signal-to-noise is near zero.

**Intervention.** An open, cross-regulator, batch-level recall and NSQ alert registry with a closed loop to dispensers and end users. Ingest and normalise every public alert source into one schema (INN, brand, strength, form, manufacturer, batch/lot or serial range, expiry, defect class, regulator, classification, action, verbatim source link): openFDA /drug/enforcement, /device/enforcement, /device/recall, /device/udi and /drug/shortages (structured JSON), WHO Medical Product Alerts, CDSCO national and state NSQ PDFs, NAFDAC, Ghana FDA, Kenya PPB, Rwanda FDA, TMDA, SAHPRA, MHRA, EMA, Health Canada, TGA. Build the two crosswalks regulators do not publish: NDC/RxCUI to drug-recall records (via RxNorm) and device product_code + brand/model text to GUDID identifiers; parse free-text lot ranges into structured matchers with confidence scores. On top: (a) instant batch lookup by typing or phone-camera barcode scan; (b) watchlists and push alerts (email/SMS/WhatsApp) by molecule, manufacturer, jurisdiction, or a pharmacy's uploaded stock or dispensing export, and for individuals a personal product list incl. implant UDIs; (c) automatic cross-border matching so a manufacturer+batch recalled in one country surfaces to subscribers in importing countries; (d) a stock-check workflow where each site acknowledges an alert and records units found/quarantined, producing the first real recall-effectiveness data; (e) a public API for POS/inventory vendors (Maisha Meds, mPharma, Field/Shelf Life); (f) registration cross-check against NAFDAC Greenbook, CDSCO SUGAM, Kenya PPB. Deterministic matching; an LLM is optional only for first-pass PDF/lot-text extraction behind a human verification queue.

**Business model.** Core alert data stays open (CC-BY); public lookup, basic alerts and the full individual/family-caregiver service are free, so the public-safety layer is never paywalled. Paid per-site tiers for independent and small-chain pharmacies, hospital groups, home-health and hospice agencies, long-term-care facilities, DME suppliers and clinics (bulk matching, affected-patient lists per recall, audit logs for accreditation and board inspections; these organisations already pay for recall handling in staff time). Wholesalers and distributors buy bulk batch screening via API; procurement agencies and NGOs (Global Fund principal recipients, MEDS, JMS, CHAG, MSF, UNICEF SD) buy supplier/batch screening; POS and e-pharmacy vendors license the API; regulators buy hosted alert distribution and effectiveness dashboards as SaaS. Later buyers: Medicaid managed-care plans and PBMs (member outreach) and manufacturers (verified recall-effectiveness reporting), the latter only under terms that never let a manufacturer suppress or delay an alert. No sale of user data; B2B tier under HIPAA business-associate terms. Early funding from USP, Gates Foundation, WHO Member State Mechanism and similar donors.

**Outcomes.** Median hours from regulator publication to delivery of a matched alert to subscribed dispensers and users (target under 24 h); share of subscribed pharmacies acknowledging and completing a stock check within 72 hours; units quarantined or returned per alert; recall-effectiveness rate (fraction of distributed batch recovered) reported back to regulators; share of Class I drug and device recalls for which at least one lot-level or UDI-level match was produced; share of alerted users confirming they were affected and reporting a completed action (stopped use, obtained replacement, contacted prescriber) within 14 days; MedWatch or national pharmacovigilance reports submitted through the tool; for B2B sites, patients identified per recall and staff hours per recall event before vs after adoption; audited post-recall dispensing of recalled lots in enrolled vs non-enrolled pharmacies; number of cross-border matches surfaced before the importing regulator published; jurisdictions and alerts normalised per month; time-to-detection of repeat offenders (manufacturers with multiple NSQ batches).

### Competitive landscape (crowdedness 4/10)

- **Inmar OneRecall (formerly RASMAS, originally Noblis).** Enterprise recall-notification and tracking platform for hospital pharmacies and manufacturers: aggregates FDA/manufacturer recall notices, pushes them to hospital departments, tracks acknowledgement, and (via a Premier Health case study) matches DSCSA receiving data to recalls to cut review time. Also runs manufacturer-side consignee notification and return kits (Inmar recall letters tell pharmacies to fax/email a response form and get an RGA). *Shortfall:* Hospital/enterprise pricing and procurement (GPO contracts), US-only sources, stops at the consignee (department acknowledgement), no patient/end-user loop, no LMIC regulators, no cross-border matching, no public API or open data. The manufacturer-side process is still letters, faxes and response forms.
- **ECRI Alerts Workflow / Alerts Tracker (with Automatch).** Membership-only hazard and recall management for health systems (launched 2003): curated alerts from FDA, manufacturers and member hospitals, assigned to staff with closure tracking; Automatch (2016) matches alerts against a hospital's equipment inventory and supply catalog. *Shortfall:* Device/hospital-centric, closed membership, no price point for an independent pharmacy, home-health agency or DME supplier, no patient-facing component, US/HIC only, nothing for drug NSQ alerts in LMICs.
- **TraceLink Digital Recalls / Targeted Recalls.** Pulls FDA notices into structured records (NDC, lot, instructions) and matches them against DSCSA EPCIS shipment/receipt data so a dispenser is only alerted for lots it actually received; manufacturer side can issue targeted notices. *Shortfall:* Only works where serialized DSCSA receipt data exists (US prescription drugs, TraceLink customers); nothing for OTC, devices, LMIC products, or patients; vendor lock-in; no public data layer; no recall-effectiveness reporting to regulators or the public.
- **EU FMD / EMVS national verification systems (EMVO + NMVOs).** Pack-level 2D-code verification at dispensing across the EU; the MAH can mark a batch 'recalled' or a product 'withdrawn' in the EU Hub so a pharmacy scan flags the pack. UK pharmacies also receive MHRA Class 1 recalls as National Patient Safety Alerts via the Central Alerting System and other classes by email, with contractual action timescales (immediate / 48 h / 5 days). *Shortfall:* Nordic guidance says a batch may be flagged only when the recall covers the whole national market, the flag is irreversible, and partial/pending recalls stay 'active'; the recall status may only be discovered at the point of dispense. Covers verification, not quality (a genuine pack later found NSQ is not flagged until the MAH acts). No patient notification, no data outside the EU/UK, and EMVS data is not open. CAS stops at NHS organisations and pharmacies, not patients.
- **Med Safety app (Uppsala Monitoring Centre / MHRA / WEB-RADR).** Free ADR-reporting app for health workers and the public; also shows safety news and alerts pushed by the national regulator and lets users keep a watch list of medicines for personalised alerts. *Shortfall:* ADR-centric; the alert feed is free-text news each regulator chooses to post, not batch-structured recall data; no lot/batch lookup, no cross-regulator or cross-border matching, no pharmacy stock-check workflow, no API, no measurement of recall completion. It is the closest LMIC incumbent and a natural distribution partner rather than a blocker.
- **Pack authentication: Sproxil, mPedigree (Goldkeys), PharmaSecure, NAFDAC MAS; Tiga Health DrugXafe (Nigeria).** Scratch-code SMS / app verification that a pack is genuine (Sproxil, mPedigree, PharmaSecure since 2009-2011, endorsed as NAFDAC's Mobile Authentication Service). Tiga's DrugXafe scans the 2D DataMatrix on serialised boxes to show approval status, recall info, expiry and price, with ADR reporting. *Shortfall:* Only covers brands that opt in and pay; verifies the pack, not the contents; a genuine batch later declared NSQ by CDSCO or PPB is invisible; single-country, proprietary; DrugXafe's recall feature only works on serialised packs, which most LMIC generics lack; none ingests other regulators' alerts or supports cross-border matching.
- **Regulator websites themselves (CDSCO monthly NSQ PDFs, NAFDAC numbered alerts, Kenya PPB, Rwanda FDA, Ghana FDA, SAHPRA, Morocco AMMPS) and the COMESA Competition Commission consumer alerts.** Each regulator posts its own recalls/NSQ lists: CDSCO publishes central and state NSQ PDFs monthly (211 batches Oct 2025, 205 Nov 2025, 167 Dec 2025) and added a 'dedicated link' because alerts were 'not easy to appreciate'; NAFDAC posts numbered Public Alerts with batch numbers (e.g. 07/2026 Dostinex batches GG3470, LG8659, GG2440); Kenya PPB issued 18/32/43 recalls in 2023/24/25 plus 14 rapid alerts; SAHPRA has a product-recalls page; Rwanda FDA posts recall PDFs; COMESA CCC re-broadcasts recalls across member states. *Shortfall:* PDF/HTML only, no machine-readable batch fields, no push channel to dispensers or patients, no cross-border propagation, no acknowledgement or effectiveness tracking, inconsistent recall counts even across the regulator's own statements, NAFDAC Greenbook has no API.
- **DeepRecall (api.deeprecall.io) and the ecosystem of openFDA/Health Canada scrapers (Apify actors, RecallCheck.net, RecallSentry, RecallGuide.org, FDA Enforcement Report e-mails).** DeepRecall: AI similarity search (text + image) over 120,000+ recalled products from CPSC, FDA, EU Safety Gate, UK OPSS, Health Canada, OECD, RappelConso and ACCC, sold to marketplaces/retailers, with REST API and MCP server. Dozens of Apify actors re-serve openFDA and Health Canada recall JSON. RecallCheck.net lists FDA drug recalls with lots; RecallSentry (Center for Recall Safety) enriches household-product recalls with lot/serial ranges; FDA offers e-mail subscriptions to the Enforcement Report. *Shortfall:* Consumer-product orientation (marketplaces), no lot-level matching for medicines, no LMIC drug regulators, no personal product lists or notification loop, no pharmacy workflow. US consumer listings are broadcast, so individual signal-to-noise is near zero; none builds the NDC/RxCUI or GUDID crosswalks.
- **Philips Respironics recall registration portal (manufacturer one-off).** Serial-number registration website and DME matching for the June 2021 CPAP/BiPAP/ventilator recall, under an FDA consent decree requiring multiple contact attempts. *Shortfall:* 'Actionable' excludes anyone who never found the portal; no public count of never-notified users; the mechanism had to be built from scratch for one recall and is now gone, so nothing persists for the next recall. Illustrates that even a well-funded, court-ordered effort lacks a standing registry of device users.
- **African pharmacy POS/inventory platforms: Maisha Meds, mPharma, RxAll.** Free/low-cost Android POS and inventory for private pharmacies and clinics with reimbursement and resupply programmes. *Shortfall:* No recall or NSQ alert feature found in any public material; they hold the stock and dispensing data but not the alert data, which makes them integration partners (API consumers) rather than competitors.

**What remains unsolved.** (1) No one normalises LMIC regulator alerts (CDSCO's 150-200 NSQ batches a month, NAFDAC numbered alerts, Kenya PPB, SAHPRA, Rwanda, Ghana, WHO) into a single batch-level schema with manufacturer, batch, expiry and defect fields; they remain PDFs and web posts, and CDSCO itself admits its alerts were hard to use. (2) No cross-border propagation: the only mechanism found is media relays (Malawi PMRA press release to COMESA CCC alert, weeks later), and Morocco's Ludiomil case shows even a national alert does not clear shelves. (3) No closed loop to dispensers outside the UK CAS/NatPSA system and US hospital GPO tools; independent and LMIC pharmacies rely on wholesaler letters, WhatsApp and newspapers. (4) No patient-level loop anywhere: NCPDP told pharmacists lot capture at dispensing is 'not currently feasible', the Ontario valsartan study (55,461 patients) found a 55% spike in hypertension ED visits after the recall, and the 2026 JMIRx Med study confirms prescriptions cannot be traced from the EHR to dispensed lots. (5) No recall-effectiveness measurement: GAO found FDA device audit checks contact only 2-10% of consignees, OIG (2011) found FDA's drug recall effectiveness procedures inadequate, and no public dataset reports the share of a recalled batch recovered. (6) No public NDC/RxCUI-to-recall or device-recall-to-GUDID crosswalk; AHRMM still has to lobby for UDI to be a required field in recall notices. (7) No repeat-offender index of manufacturers with multiple NSQ batches across jurisdictions, which procurement agencies (MEDS, JMS, CHAG, Global Fund PRs) would use for supplier screening. (8) Authentication systems (Sproxil, mPedigree, FMD, DrugXafe) answer 'is this pack genuine?' but never 'has this genuine batch been declared substandard?'.

**Differentiated wedge.** Be the batch-and-manufacturer registry that follows medicines across borders: ingest CDSCO central+state NSQ PDFs, NAFDAC, Kenya PPB, SAHPRA, Rwanda FDA, Ghana FDA, COMESA CCC and WHO alerts into one schema keyed on manufacturer + product + batch, then (a) surface every alert against a manufacturer to subscribers in importing countries the same day (the India-to-Africa export channel and the Kenya-to-COMESA channel, where nothing exists today), (b) publish a cross-jurisdiction repeat-offender index of manufacturers with multiple NSQ/recalled batches that procurement agencies and faith-based supply organisations (MEDS, JMS, CHAG), Global Fund principal recipients and wholesalers pay for as supplier screening, and (c) give every subscribing site a 'stock check' acknowledgement that records units found/quarantined, producing the first public recall-effectiveness dataset, which regulators and donors (USP, WHO Member State Mechanism) have never had. Deliver alerts over WhatsApp/SMS/email and an API consumed by POS vendors (Maisha Meds, mPharma) and the Med Safety app rather than building a consumer app. Keep openFDA + AccessGUDID + RxNorm as the HIC tier (independent pharmacies, home-health, caregivers with implant UDIs) to prove the schema and crosswalks, but do not lead with it. The defensibility is the normalised historical corpus plus the acknowledgement data, neither of which an openFDA scraper or a pack-authentication vendor can replicate.

## At the pharmacy counter patients cannot see where a medicine is in stock, what an acceptable substitute is, or the regulated price of the cheapest equivalent

*Id:* `point-of-sale-medicine-availability-price` · *Merged from:* When a medicine is out of stock, patients and clinicians have no way to learn where it is ; Patients pay several times the regulated or reference price for the same molecule because 

**Who.** LMIC patients paying out of pocket at public and private outlets: lowest-priced generics are available in only ~38% of public facilities in the WHO Africa region (Lancet Global Health 2025 pooled analysis); Kenya Apr-May 2025: >40% of facilities reported stockouts of critical medicines and nearly two-thirds of Kenyans paid out of pocket; women needing SRH commodities in Kenya, Tanzania, Uganda and Zambia face 3-12 stockout days per month per facility. Indian patients prescribed branded generics (100-1,177% price variation between brands of the same molecule and strength; ~34% of 1,575 antihypertensive brands priced above the DPCO 2021 ceiling; only 12% of 200 patients in a 2024 study knew the branded/generic difference). The poorest quintile globally (3 in 4 face financial hardship from health costs vs fewer than 1 in 25 of the richest, WHO/World Bank 2025). In high-income countries: people with ADHD (US survey of 10,936: 38% could not fill, 21% still disrupted; patients call 3-20 pharmacies a month), GLP-1, cancer and ICU patients, and community pharmacists in 27 EU/EFTA countries spending ~11 hours a week hunting stock and substitutes.

**Scale.** Availability: Lancet Global Health 2025 (71 WHO/HAI surveys, 54 countries) found public-sector availability of lowest-priced generics from 37.6% (Africa) to 68.4% (Europe), with patients paying 3.0-11.5x international reference prices for generics and >25x for originators. Uganda (2017 working paper): private antimalarial prices rise 36% during public stockouts and low-income buyers drop out. USA: ASHP recorded 323 active shortages in Q1 2024, the most since 2001; 270 in March 2025; 41% of active shortages have lasted >2 years. Europe: PGEU 2025 report, 96% of responding countries report shortages, 89% report treatment interruptions; pharmacist time on shortages rose from 6.3 h/week (2020) to ~11 (2024). Affordability: WHO/World Bank 2025 Global Monitoring Report, 2.1 billion people (26%) faced financial hardship from out-of-pocket health spending in 2022; medicines are 20-60% of health spending in LMICs and >60% of OOP health spending in WHO South-East Asia. India: NPPA demanded ~Rs 10,013 crore (~US$1.2bn) for over-ceiling sales, 85% unrecovered as of 30 Sept 2025; Jan Aushadhi (17,990 outlets by Dec 2025) sells 50-80% below branded prices. Caveats: Kenya figures rest on a single news source; the share of hardship attributable to opacity vs poverty, and a global death toll from stockouts, are not quantified.

**Why solutions fail.** Official shortage lists (openFDA, ASHP, EMA catalogue, BfArM, national EU registers) say what is short nationally, never where stock physically is, and mostly depend on voluntary manufacturer reports. Pharmacy inventory is proprietary, so patients and prescribers phone around; locators need pharmacy opt-in feeds (ApoApp Austria, ~1,000 of 1,400 pharmacies after years), charge patients for staff to phone pharmacies (Medfinder US), or are local (InStockRx NJ, ScriptChecker UK). African pharmacy-tech (Field/Shelf Life, Maisha Meds, mPharma) sells supply tools to pharmacies, not availability to patients; Medsaf shut in March 2024. Uganda mTrac SMS reporting (~5,000 facilities; ACT stockouts fell 25.2% to 13.8% by 2012) feeds the ministry, not the counter. Regulated price data are unusable at the counter: South Africa's Medicine Price Registry is public but hard to use; India's NPPA Pharma Sahi Daam covers only scheduled medicines with no working overcharge loop (85% of dues unrecovered); the Philippines DPRI has no public download; Brazil's public list found was dated 2019. E-pharmacies (Tata 1mg, PharmEasy, TrueMeds) show substitutes but are urban, online and sales-driven; GoodRx is US-only. Substitution knowledge sits in pharmacists' heads; brand prescribing, pharmacist margins on brands and quality fears (reinforced by real NSQ incidents) persist.

**Intervention.** An open point-of-sale medicine engine answering three questions for one search by brand or molecule: where is it in stock, what is an acceptable substitute, and what is the regulated price of the cheapest equivalent. (1) Ingest official shortage feeds (openFDA, EMA catalogue, national EU registers, BfArM, UK SSPs) and regulated price lists (India NPPA ceilings and Jan Aushadhi MRPs, South Africa SEP, Philippines DPRI, Brazil CMED, Kenya KEMSA/MEDS, HAI/MSH reference prices), mapped to RxNorm/ATC. (2) An equivalence engine from public data (RxNorm, Orange Book TE codes, NDC, WHO EML/ATC-DDD, CDSCO SUGAM, SAHPRA, PPB registers) enumerating equivalent presentations, salts, forms and registered brands with MRPs, flagging same-class alternatives for prescriber decision, linked to NSQ/recall registries to answer quality fears with data. (3) Time-decayed, de-duplicated "filled here today at this price" sightings from patients, CHWs and verified pharmacists, with receipt-photo moderation, trust scoring, no personal health data, controlled substances shown only as "recently filled" with rate limits. (4) A prescriber substitution packet (equivalent, dose conversion, ceiling price, transfer note) and a one-tap pre-filled overcharge complaint to the regulator. (5) Offline-first PWA plus SMS/USSD; opt-in POS inventory feeds for pharmacies. Useful without pharmacy integration; no AI needed for core functions.

**Business model.** Free for patients, caregivers and community health workers. Revenue from clinics and health systems (substitution and generic-prescribing tools, EHR/e-prescribing integration), health insurers, government schemes and microinsurers (claims price-audit and benchmark API), pharmacies that opt into verified-status and receipt-verified "fair price" badges (strict no-pay-to-rank, sighting-based verification), payers/PBMs and manufacturers (aggregated, anonymised shortage-impact and abandonment data), and public-health agencies, regulators and NGOs licensing availability and price-monitoring dashboards in place of ad hoc surveys. In LMICs, contracts with ministries, Global Fund/PEPFAR implementing partners and NGOs for facility-level monitoring; grant funding plausible from HAI, WHO and Gates. No pharmaceutical sponsorship, no sale of individual patient or purchase data; crowd data stays open under a share-alike licence.

**Outcomes.** Days from prescription to fill for shortage-listed drugs (users vs matched baseline); pharmacies contacted per successful fill; sighting precision (confirmed-in-stock rate when a user acts within 24 h); prescription abandonment rate for shortage drugs via pharmacy partners; clinician minutes per shortage episode; receipt-verified per-prescription out-of-pocket cost before vs after use; share of searches where a cheaper registered equivalent was purchased; number and resolution rate of overcharge complaints filed; price dispersion for tracked molecules in monitored cities over time; refill continuity and self-reported treatment-interruption days for chronic patients (hypertension, diabetes) vs controls; in LMICs, facility stockout-days reported, median time to resolution, and share of patients redirected to an in-stock facility within a set distance; molecules and countries with regulated-price coverage; insurer claim savings on audited prescriptions.

### Competitive landscape (crowdedness 5/10)

- **Medfinder (US) and its concierge clones: Needle, FindRx, US MedicineFinder, MedHound.** Paid human/AI concierge: patient enters drug, dose and location; staff (or, for Needle, an AI caller) phone pharmacies and report which one has stock. Tiers reported at $50 for one search, $120 for three, $180 for six; money-back if nothing is found. Needle (Draper, Utah) does the same for ADHD and GLP-1 drugs on per-use or subscription pricing. *Shortfall:* Linear human cost per search (the price is the proof that no data layer exists); US-only; reviewers report pharmacies listed as in-stock that were not, out-of-network pharmacies, and having to repeat the hunt every 30 days; pharmacies routinely refuse to confirm controlled-substance stock by phone; no substitution or price guidance; nothing accumulates into a reusable dataset.
- **ApoApp (Austrian Chamber of Pharmacists), preceded by Kwizda's ApoScout.** Official chamber app showing real-time stock (incl. pack sizes) at nearby pharmacies, refreshed every 10 minutes from pharmacy inventory systems; plus leaflets, opening hours, reminders. Health hotline 1450 relays the same data by phone. *Shortfall:* Only works because one chamber could negotiate POS feeds in one small, uniformly-priced market; years to reach ~70% coverage; no substitution logic, no shortage-feed linkage, no price comparison (not needed in Austria), not replicable where pharmacies are fragmented or hostile to sharing inventory (US, India, Africa).
- **KNMP Farmanco (Netherlands) and CISMED (Spain): pharmacist-reported shortage and alternatives registries.** Farmanco (since 2004) lists unavailable registered medicines, cause, expected return date and the alternative used; entries come from pharmacists, wholesalers and suppliers. CISMED aggregates stock-incident reports from Spanish community pharmacies via the colleges to the national council. *Shortfall:* Both are national, professional-facing, single-language tools: they say what is short and what pharmacists substituted, not which pharmacy has it; CISMED data flow to the council and regulator, not to patients; no price layer; no API for third parties; substitution knowledge stays inside the profession.
- **Official shortage registers: openFDA/ASHP (US), EMA ESMP and shortages catalogue (EU), BfArM/PharmNet.Bund (DE), NHSBSA SSPs (UK), TGA (AU), Health Product Shortages Canada.** Manufacturer- or pharmacist-reported national shortage listings. openFDA exposes a JSON API refreshed daily; ESMP became mandatory for MAHs on 2 Feb 2025 with a public page; TGA offers a CSV of all active entries; Canada has a token-based REST API (1,000 req/h). *Shortfall:* National-level status only, no location; rely on voluntary or MAH reporting (a 2022 Australian survey found 29% of shortages pharmacists experienced were absent from the TGA database); EMA catalogue and Union list have no documented CSV/API; German database moved domains (lieferengpass.bfarm.de to anwendungen.pharmnet-bund.de) and export is undocumented; none links to equivalents, prices or pharmacies.
- **Pharma Sahi Daam 2.0 + Pharma Jan Samadhan (NPPA, India).** Government app/web lookup of DPCO ceiling prices for scheduled formulations by name or salt, Hindi/English, brand comparison, overcharge complaint filing; integrated with the Jan Samadhan grievance portal (June 2026). *Shortfall:* Covers only scheduled (NLEM) medicines, so most of the 100-1,177% inter-brand price spread on non-scheduled drugs is invisible; no stock information; no Jan Aushadhi cross-reference; complaint loop is slow and poorly enforced; ceiling data are published as per-notification web pages and PDFs, not a maintained dataset.
- **Jan Aushadhi Sugam (PMBI/BPPI, India).** Locates the nearest Jan Aushadhi Kendra on Google Maps, searches the PMBJP generic basket (2,047 medicines, 300 surgicals as of 2025) and shows Jan Aushadhi MRP vs branded MRP. *Shortfall:* Only PMBJP products and outlets; does not map an arbitrary prescribed brand to its generic; stock at a specific kendra is not reliably shown (third-party guides claim it, official material does not); no private-pharmacy coverage; no ceiling-price or overcharge link.
- **Indian e-pharmacy substitution engines: TrueMeds, Medkart, Tata 1mg, PharmEasy.** Upload prescription; a staff doctor proposes a cheaper same-composition brand and the e-pharmacy delivers it. Medkart adds a brand-vs-generic price comparison engine and ~100 physical stores. Tata 1mg lists branded and generic versions but will not substitute when the prescription names a brand. *Shortfall:* Sales-driven and online-only: substitution is a conversion lever, not open reference data; urban delivery footprint; nothing for the patient standing at an offline counter; no regulated-price or NSQ-quality layer; brand-to-molecule databases are proprietary (the only open approximations are scraped CSVs of unknown licence).
- **Medicine Price Registry / medicineprices.org.za (OpenUp, ex-Code4SA, South Africa).** Civic-tech lookup of the Department of Health Single Exit Price list: search a medicine, see the maximum price, generics with the same active ingredient and the allowed dispensing fee. *Shortfall:* One country, price-only, no stock or substitution reasoning beyond same ingredient; site itself warns dispensing-fee rules change and it 'may not be completely up-to-date'; volunteer-maintained; the SEP spreadsheet's logistics fee column does not reflect actual paid amounts (Daily Maverick, Feb 2026).
- **Surescripts Real-Time Prescription Benefit (US).** Inside the EHR at the moment of prescribing, shows the insured patient's expected copay at the chosen pharmacy, prior-auth flags and covered lower-cost alternatives. *Shortfall:* Insured US patients only; shows benefit price, not stock or shortage status; closed network, no public API; irrelevant to cash-pay and LMIC patients; alternatives are formulary-driven, not shortage-driven.
- **Pharmacy2U Medicine Stock Checker (UK) and ScriptChecker.** Free, no-login lookup by medicine and dose returning in stock / out of stock / discontinued for Pharmacy2U's own warehouse; ScriptChecker (Epsom, founded 2023) proposes an app for pharmacies to publish low-stock availability. *Shortfall:* Single-retailer stock, so an 'out of stock' answer just sends you back to phoning local pharmacies; no substitutes; ScriptChecker depends on pharmacy opt-in and shows no traction.
- **InStockRx (New Jersey, with Garden State Pharmacy Owners).** Independent pharmacies opt in to expose selected inventory (cold/flu, diabetes) online; also pharmacy-to-pharmacy surplus transfer and generic suggestions. *Shortfall:* Single state, limited drug categories, opt-in only; no evidence it covers ADHD or shortage-listed drugs; no price or regulated-price layer.
- **African pharmacy-tech: mPharma, Maisha Meds, Field/Shelf Life, Remedial Health, DrugStoc (and failed Medsaf); consumer attempts PharmaLoop (NG), Drug Spot (CM), GoGo Plus (GH).** B2B inventory, consignment and procurement tools sold to pharmacies (mPharma ~1.5M patients/year across 4 countries via its franchise; Maisha Meds building a 'digital PBM' for LMICs). PharmaLoop claims a WhatsApp-first stock-check-and-reserve network; Drug Spot and GoGo Plus are small price/availability comparison apps. *Shortfall:* The funded players sell to pharmacies and have no incentive to publish stock or prices to patients; consumer apps are unverified or tiny; no linkage to KEMSA/MEDS reference prices, PPB registers or NSQ alerts; no SMS/USSD reach; no CHW channel.

**What remains unsolved.** Nobody answers the three counter questions together for one search. Stock locators are either paid phone concierges (Medfinder, Needle), single-retailer checkers (Pharmacy2U), or chamber/opt-in feeds that took years to reach partial coverage in one small market (ApoApp). Shortage registers are national-only, rely on voluntary or MAH reporting, and in most jurisdictions have no usable export. Equivalence knowledge exists in pharmacists' heads and in professional registries (Farmanco shows 73% of shortages are solvable with a same-active product) but is never surfaced per presentation, linked to shortage status at NDC/pack level, or exposed to prescribers and patients. Regulated prices are published by regulators as PDFs and notification pages (NPPA), civic-tech lookups (South Africa) or monthly spreadsheets (Brazil CMED), with no brand-to-molecule-to-ceiling join and no enforcement loop (85% of NPPA dues unrecovered). In India the brand-to-molecule map is proprietary (e-pharmacies, Fiverr sellers), so substitution is only offered as a sales funnel. In sub-Saharan Africa the funded pharmacy-tech is pharmacy-facing and the consumer attempts are unverified. Crowdsourced stock has never worked at scale because stock is perishable, pharmacies refuse to confirm controlled substances, and nobody has solved cold start or fraud. The structural blocker, pharmacy inventory being proprietary and pharmacies having no incentive to share, is not solved by any incumbent and will not be solved by software alone.

**Differentiated wedge.** A 'fillable alternatives' engine for shortage-listed medicines, sold first to prescribers and pharmacists, not a consumer stock map. For any drug in an official shortage feed, enumerate every presentation (strength, salt, form, pack) and AB-rated or same-active equivalent using Orange Book TE codes, RxNorm and DailyMed, and flag which of those presentations are themselves in shortage at NDC level and which are not; attach dose-conversion notes, the regulated or reference price where one exists, and a one-click substitution packet (transfer note, prescriber letter, SSP/regulatory citation). This is the one layer none of the twelve incumbents provides: Farmanco knows alternatives but is NL-only and pharmacist-only, Surescripts knows price but not shortage, Medfinder knows stock but not substitutes. Distribution comes through pharmacists and clinics who already spend hours on this, which also seeds the only kind of stock signal that has ever worked: verified pharmacist 'filled here today' sightings rather than anonymous crowd reports. Launch with non-controlled, high-volume shortage classes (GLP-1s, antibiotics, oncology generics, ADHD non-stimulants, insulin presentations) and exclude Schedule II publicly. Country adapters then bolt the regulated-price question onto the same equivalence spine: India (NPPA ceiling + Jan Aushadhi MRP + CDSCO NSQ + pre-filled Jan Samadhan complaint), South Africa (SEP), Brazil (CMED), with international reference prices and KEMSA/MEDS lists as the degraded mode. Revenue from clinics, pharmacies and payers for the substitution and audit API; patient and CHW views free; crowd and equivalence data published under a share-alike licence so the engine becomes infrastructure rather than another list.

## No one owns the older adult's complete medication list and care plan across prescribers and the hospital-to-home transition

*Id:* `older-adult-care-continuity` · *Merged from:* Medication harm in older adults from polypharmacy and fall-risk drugs because no one owns ; The hospital-to-home transition for older adults: family caregivers sent home with unreada

**Who.** Adults 65+ on five or more medicines, especially those seeing several specialists plus a GP or recently discharged from hospital, and the family members who manage their pills and take over care the afternoon they come home. A 2024 meta-analysis (122 studies, 57.3 million people) puts polypharmacy at nearly 40% of older adults worldwide, highest in Europe, North America, age 70+ and nursing homes; it is 62% among older people with dementia (2023 pooled estimate). In the US more than 4 in 10 older adults take 5+ prescriptions (Lown Institute); in England over 1 million people take 10+ medicines, almost half aged 75+ (Feb 2026 policy brief). The person doing the work is usually a family caregiver: roughly 20 million US caregivers perform medical/nursing tasks (wound care, injections, drains, complex regimens), 82% of them manage medications, most with no training, and only about 20% received 24 hours' notice of discharge (AARP Home Alone Revisited 2019). The 2025 AARP/NAC survey counts 63 million US caregivers, 44% providing high-intensity care and 38% with no planning in place. Hardest hit: people 75+, people with dementia or cognitive impairment, patients with heart failure, COPD or hip fracture, and caregivers with low health literacy or limited English. The same failures are documented in the NHS, Canada and Australia.

**Scale.** US: an estimated 10 million older Americans (1 in 5) had an adverse drug event in 2018 and ~280,000 were hospitalized for one (Lown Institute, 2019). ED visits for harm from correctly used medicines reach 16 per 1,000 people aged 75+ per year, led by anticoagulant bleeding and hypoglycemia (Budnitz et al., JAMA 2021). A Korean cohort of adults 65-84 (2016-2018) found continuous polypharmacy associated with adjusted hazard ratios of 1.85 for hospitalization, 1.92 for ED visits and 2.57 for death (Chae et al., 2024). WHO (2022) states half of all preventable harm in care is medication-related, with global cost of medication errors about US$42 billion a year. Falls are the main injury pathway: CDC reports 41,400 fall deaths among Americans 65+ in 2023 (69.9 per 100,000, rising since 2003); 94% of adults 65+ took at least one high-risk medication in 2017 vs 57% in 1999; 45.8% of older adults presenting to an ED after a fall were on fall-risk-increasing drugs (2022). The transition is where harm concentrates: medication-related harm affects 17-51% of older patients within 30 days of discharge, 35-59% preventable (Parekh et al., 2018); at 30 days nearly half were nonadherent to at least one in-hospital medication change, 26% had visited an ED and 6% were readmitted (Weir et al., 2020). Medicare FFS 30-day readmissions were 15.0% in 2023 (MedPAC, Jan 2025); 42% of hospitals were penalized under HRRP in FY2023. 81% of standard discharge documents exceed sixth-grade reading level (Am J Med 2025). Pooled RCTs show caregiver integration in discharge planning cuts readmission 25% at 90 days (RR 0.75; Rodakowski et al., JAGS 2017). Lown projects 4.6 million hospitalizations and 150,000 premature deaths over the 2020s if nothing changes (a projection). (Note: some primary pages were read via search excerpts because direct fetches were blocked.)

**Why solutions fail.** The tools exist but are applied by the wrong people at the wrong moment. Explicit deprescribing criteria (AGS Beers; STOPP/START v3, 2023, 190 criteria, CC-BY) are implemented as EHR alerts to prescribers who suffer alert fatigue, and each prescriber sees only their own slice of the list; reviews of deprescribing barriers (BJGP 2020) find fragmented care, GP-specialist communication gaps, time pressure, and deprescribing only after harm. Formal reviews reach few people: only ~3.2 million of ~50 million Medicare Part D enrollees (6.4%) received a Comprehensive Medication Review in 2022, and England's Structured Medication Reviews are restricted to 10+ medicines and average 0.9 changes per review. Fall-related encounters do not reduce FRID use afterwards. Consumer apps (Medisafe and similar) are adherence tools: they help people take pills on time, not ask whether a pill should still be there, and the free NLM drug-interaction API was discontinued in January 2024. On the transition side, everything is hospital- or clinician-facing: discharge platforms (CarePort/WellSky, EHR modules) optimize bed flow, not what the family does at 8 pm on day one. Transitional Care Management pays for contact within two business days, but uptake is low and skewed away from the highest-risk, minority and frail beneficiaries (2015-2019 claims), and the clinician has no view of what happened at home. The CARE Act (44 states and territories) requires hospitals to record and instruct a caregiver, but compliance is weakly measured and 'instruction' is a verbal walkthrough at the door. Discharge summaries are written for the next clinician; caregiver proxy portal access is patchy. The first 72 hours, when medication changes, new tasks and red-flag symptoms all land at once, have no owner.

**Intervention.** A caregiver-owned care-continuity ledger: one canonical medication list and a 30-day home operating plan, built from structured data and run by explicit, citable rules. The family caregiver and older adult assemble the list across all prescribers and pharmacies (manual entry with RxNorm autocomplete; optional pill-label photo; optional import via CMS Blue Button 2.0 claims or the hospital's Cures Act Patient Access FHIR API: MedicationRequest, Condition, Procedure, Appointment). The backend normalizes items to RxCUI/ATC and runs transparent screens rather than opaque advice: STOPP v3 criteria, anticholinergic burden scoring, fall-risk-increasing drug classes, therapeutic duplication via RxClass, drug-disease flags against entered conditions, renal-dose flags if eGFR is entered. Outputs: (1) a one-page 'questions for your prescriber or pharmacist' sheet naming each criterion and its evidence; (2) event triggers that assemble a review request on a fall, discharge or new prescriber ('a fall on 3 March; these three medicines are FRIDs'); (3) a versioned shareable list for ED and admission; (4) on discharge, a day-by-day 30-day schedule built around new, stopped and changed-dose medicines with plain-language reasons, skilled tasks (wound care, injections, drains) mapped to the free AARP Home Alone Alliance videos, follow-up appointments with 'what to bring and ask', condition-specific green/yellow/red symptom zones with a who-to-call ladder so the ED is the last step, a CARE Act rights checklist and 'ask for a TCM visit' prompt; (5) a daily status log (weight, symptoms, doses) viewable by the home-health nurse or TCM clinician via share link. Novelty: it is owned by the person who sees all the bottles, converts criteria clinicians ignore into patient-side requests prescribers must answer, fires on the events the system misses, and operationalizes the one transition intervention with RCT evidence (caregiver integration) without adding hospital staff. Useful with zero AI: a rule engine over open criteria plus FHIR mapping and condition templates; OCR and free-text parsing are optional later.

**Business model.** B2B2C with the family tier free. Buyers whose money is already at stake for ADE admissions and readmissions and whose quality scores reward reviews: Medicare Advantage and Part D plans (CMR completion is a Star Ratings measure; the tool prepares and documents reviews), ACOs and hospitals carrying HRRP penalties and needing CARE Act compliance evidence and billable TCM 99495/99496 visits, PACE and GUIDE/chronic-care-management providers, home care and home health agencies (start-of-care efficiency), UK Primary Care Networks that must deliver SMRs and ICB discharge-to-assess programs, long-term care insurers, and employer caregiver benefits. Pricing per enrolled member per month, per discharge, or per completed documented review. Incentives are aligned because the buyer only wins when fewer high-risk medicines, fewer ADE admissions and fewer readmissions occur; no pharma funding, no data sales, no device tie-ins.

**Outcomes.** Verified deprescribing: number and share of flagged medicines discontinued or dose-reduced within 90 days (caregiver-confirmed, validated against claims in a pilot); change in anticholinergic burden score per person; FRID count 90 days after a logged fall; share of falls followed by a documented medication review; completed CMRs/SMRs per 1,000 members. Transition outcomes: 30- and 90-day readmission and ED return rates in pilot discharges versus matched controls; medication-change discrepancies found at a day-7 reconciliation; share of discharges where the family had the plan at least 24 hours before leaving; TCM contact within 2 business days and visit within 14 days; caregiver Preparedness for Caregiving Scale at day 7; share of yellow-zone escalations resolved by phone rather than ED; CARE Act compliance documentation rate; falls, ED visits and ADE hospitalizations per 100 person-years versus matched controls. Not counted as success: medications listed, plans generated, logins, pages viewed, or reminders sent.

### Competitive landscape (crowdedness 6/10)

- **Medisafe (consumer adherence app).** Pill reminders, a medication list, refill alerts and 'Medfriend' caregiver notifications; free tier plus ~$4.99/mo premium; increasingly monetized through pharma patient-support programs and a 2025 voice agent (VIA). *Shortfall:* It is an adherence tool: it helps people take what is prescribed, never asks whether a drug should still be there, runs no STOPP/Beers/anticholinergic/fall-risk screens, has no discharge-change reconciliation or 30-day home plan, and its revenue now comes from pharma, which is structurally opposed to deprescribing.
- **Arine (AI medication intelligence for health plans).** Claims-and-EHR-driven medication optimization platform that generates pharmacist/care-manager work queues (gaps, PIMs, adherence, cost) for Medicare Advantage and Medicaid plans. *Shortfall:* Entirely plan/pharmacist-facing: the older adult and family never see the list or the reasoning, there is no artifact to carry into the ED or home, nothing fires on a fall or a discharge the plan has not yet seen in claims (30-90 day lag), and outcome numbers are vendor-reported with no independent evaluation found.
- **FeelBetter (AI polypharmacy for ACOs/health systems).** Risk-stratifies polypharmacy patients from EHR data and surfaces prioritized medication-change recommendations to clinicians and pharmacists. *Shortfall:* Another clinician-side alert generator competing for the same alert-fatigued prescriber attention the opportunity brief identifies as the failure point; no caregiver role, no transition plan, no event triggers from home.
- **MedSafer (academic electronic deprescribing decision support, McGill/MUHC).** Cross-references a hospitalized or long-term-care patient's medications and conditions against STOPP, Beers and Choosing Wisely to produce a deprescribing report for the treating team. *Shortfall:* The best-evidenced clinician tool shows the ceiling of the 'alert the prescriber in hospital' model: more deprescribing, no ADE benefit, and nothing that follows the patient home where 17-51% of harm occurs. It is not commercial at scale and has no patient/caregiver interface.
- **Synapse Medicine (medication intelligence and reconciliation, France/US).** API and clinician tools for drug information, prescription analysis and medication reconciliation at care transitions; consumer-facing 'Yuka of medicine' drug lookup in France. *Shortfall:* Sold to software vendors, hospitals and pharmacies; the reconciliation happens inside institutions, not in the household; no caregiver ownership, no 30-day plan, no event-triggered reviews; US presence still early.
- **Cureatr / SinfoniaRx (now technology owned by AssureCare).** Pharmacist-staffed transitions-of-care medication management clinic ('Meds 360°') plus MTM services for plans; Cureatr bought SinfoniaRx from Tabula Rasa in March 2023, then sold both technologies to AssureCare in June 2024. *Shortfall:* Human-pharmacist service model that scales with headcount; the patient receives a phone call, not a persistent canonical list; consolidation shows that B2B medication services alone did not produce a durable standalone business.
- **Outcomes (formerly OutcomesMTM) and Aspen RxHealth (Part D MTM / CMR vendors).** Deliver Medicare Part D Medication Therapy Management, primarily the annual telephone or in-pharmacy Comprehensive Medication Review that counts toward the CMS Star measure. *Shortfall:* Eligibility is plan-defined (multiple chronic conditions, 8+ drugs, cost threshold), the review is calendar-driven not event-driven, it depends on a pharmacist reaching the member by phone, the family caregiver is not the counterparty, and the output is a mailed summary rather than a living list.
- **Laguna Health (post-discharge recovery app, pivoted to contextual care management).** Launched 2021 as a patient app plus coaches for the 30 days after discharge (NorthShore pilot); by 2023 repositioned as an AI 'contextual care' platform for payer and provider care-management teams. *Shortfall:* The pivot away from the consumer recovery app toward care-manager tooling is the most important market signal in this space: direct patient recovery apps struggled to get distribution. It is coach-led, not criteria-driven, has no canonical medication list and no deprescribing logic.
- **Kouper Health (AI transitions-of-care outreach).** Generative-AI SMS and phone outreach after discharge to schedule follow-ups, route patients to programs and capture data for hospitals and payers. *Shortfall:* Hospital-side engagement and scheduling automation; it does not own the medication list, does not reason over criteria, does not equip the family at home, and its customer (the hospital) loses sight of the patient after the follow-up is booked.
- **CipherHealth, Memora Health (acquired by Commure, Dec 2024) and Vital's Discharge Assist (hospital post-discharge engagement vendors).** Automated post-discharge calls/texts, care-navigation messaging, and AI-simplified discharge checklists sold to hospitals and health systems. *Shortfall:* Optimized for bed flow, HCAHPS and penalty avoidance; scripts are generic, stop at day 30, do not reconcile medications across prescribers, and nothing is owned by or portable to the family. Memora's exit price and Laguna's pivot show how hard this segment is for independents.
- **Wellthy, Carallel, ianacare (employer/plan caregiver-support benefits).** Human care coordinators plus apps that help working family caregivers with logistics (finding care, insurance, paperwork), sold as an employer or health-plan benefit. *Shortfall:* Generalist concierge model; no structured medication record, no clinical criteria, no FHIR/claims import, and distribution limited to employees of large employers, which excludes most 75+ households and most spousal caregivers.
- **NHS Discharge Medicines Service (England) and comparable programs (US TCM billing, Ontario MedsCheck, Australian Home Medicines Review).** Hospital refers discharged patients to a community pharmacy, which is paid (£12 + £12) to compare discharge medicines with pre-admission medicines and counsel the patient. *Shortfall:* Works only where the hospital remembers to refer and the pharmacy has staff; the family is not a participant and gets no durable artifact; it is a one-time reconciliation, not an owner of the first 30 days or of falls afterward. US TCM has the same uptake and equity problems.
- **CareZone (consumer medication-list app; tech bought by Walmart 2020) and Jointly (Carers UK).** CareZone let families photograph bottles to build a shared medication list and calendar; Walmart bought its IP for a reported ~$200M in 2020 and retired the successor Wellness app on 31 Jan 2023. Jointly is a £2.99 'circle of care' app with messaging, tasks and a medication list. *Shortfall:* Both prove the demand for a family-owned list and the failure to monetize it directly: static lists with no screening logic, no clinical buyer, no event triggers. CareZone's retirement left no large consumer-owned medication list in the US market.
- **Hero, MedMinder (connected pill dispensers) and free interaction checkers (Drugs.com, WebMD, GoodRx).** Hardware dispensers with caregiver apps ($30-60/month plus device fee, some Medicare-covered programs); web interaction checkers that flag drug-drug interactions for any entered list. *Shortfall:* Dispensers are adherence hardware (lower feasibility for a small software team and irrelevant to 'should this pill exist'); interaction checkers are drug-pair only, ignore age-specific criteria, conditions, falls, renal function, therapeutic duplication and discharge changes, and produce no plan or clinician-facing request.

**What remains unsolved.** Every funded player attacks the problem from the institution's side: Arine, FeelBetter, Synapse and MedSafer generate alerts for prescribers or plan pharmacists; Kouper, CipherHealth, Memora/Commure and Vital automate hospital outreach; Outcomes/Aspen deliver a calendar-driven annual CMR that reaches ~6% of Part D members; the NHS DMS reaches only hospitals that refer. The best-evidenced clinician tool (MedSafer RCT, 5,698 patients) increased deprescribing but did not reduce 30-day adverse drug events, which is consistent with the brief's claim that the moment of failure is after the patient goes home. No product in the market gives the person who sees all the bottles a canonical, versioned, criteria-screened list; nothing fires a review request on the events the system misses (a fall, a new prescriber, an ED visit); nothing reconciles discharge changes (new/stopped/changed doses) against the pre-admission list at the household level; and nothing turns the first 72 hours and 30 days at home into an operating plan the family can run. Consumer products that tried the household list (CareZone, Walmart Wellness, Jointly) either died or stayed tiny because they sold to families instead of to the parties already paying for readmissions and CMRs; Laguna's pivot shows the direct-to-patient recovery app also lacked distribution. Two technical gaps remain genuinely unsolved for anyone building this: (1) there is no free, commercially licensed drug-drug interaction dataset since NLM shut its API in January 2024 (DDInter 2.0 and deprescribing.org algorithms are CC BY-NC-SA; TWOSIDES is pharmacovigilance signal data, not clinical guidance; AGS Beers is copyrighted and licensed), and (2) patient-access FHIR onboarding is per-vendor and slow, so manual entry plus claims import is the realistic path for an MVP.

**Differentiated wedge.** Own the 'discharge reconciliation packet' for the family, and sell the documented output to the clinician who gets paid for the visit. Concretely: on discharge (or after a fall), the caregiver builds the canonical list in under 20 minutes with RxNorm autocomplete plus optional Blue Button Part D import, marks which items were new/stopped/dose-changed on the discharge paperwork, and the rules engine (STOPP v3 CC-BY criteria, STOPPFall FRID classes, anticholinergic burden, RxClass therapeutic duplication, MED-RT drug-disease flags against entered conditions, openFDA boxed warnings) produces three artifacts: a one-page discrepancy-and-questions sheet that cites the criterion for each line, a versioned shareable list for the ED, and a day-by-day 30-day home plan with condition zone sheets and linked AARP task videos. The share link lands in the hands of whoever is already paid or scored to act: the home health nurse at start of care (OASIS drug regimen review), the TCM 99495/99496 clinician inside 2 business days, the Part D MTM pharmacist (CMR documentation for Stars MY2027), or the NHS DMS/SMR pharmacist. The product's unit of value is 'a documented, pre-populated medication review triggered by a real event', which no competitor produces from the household side and which the buyer can bill or report. Deliberately de-scope drug-drug interactions, Beers and NLP of discharge PDFs at MVP (licensing and accuracy risks), lead with the criteria that are open and explainable, keep all outputs as review prompts with the clinician as decision-maker, and make the family tier free so the list exists before the buyer arrives.

## Family caregivers (especially of people with dementia) are unidentified, untrained and unconnected to individualized support

*Id:* `family-caregiver-support-gap` · *Merged from:* Family caregivers managing dementia behavioral symptoms at home with no individualized sup; Family caregivers burn out invisibly: they are rarely identified by the health system, rar

**Who.** Unpaid family caregivers of older, disabled or chronically ill adults, above all those caring at home for someone with dementia; about two-thirds are women, concentrated at ages 45-64, with a growing share under 50 (one in three US caregivers; 47% of under-50 carers are 'sandwich generation'). US: 63 million caregivers, about 1 in 4 adults (AARP/NAC, July 2025); nearly 12 million care for someone with dementia, 66% live with the person and 60% were employed in the past year (Alzheimer's Association 2025 Facts and Figures). Europe: informal carers provide about 80% of all long-term care (Eurocarers). UK: 57% of carers feel frequently overwhelmed and 35% report poor mental health, up from 27% a year earlier (Carers UK State of Caring 2024). Globally, WHO (EB156, 2025) counts 57 million people with dementia in 2021, over 60% in LMICs, nearly 10 million new cases a year, roughly two-thirds living in the community; 75% are undiagnosed (about 41 million; up to 90% in LMICs, ADI World Alzheimer Report 2021), so most families manage behavioral symptoms without any diagnosis-triggered support.

**Scale.** US caregivers rose 45% (nearly 20 million) since 2015; 24% provide 40+ hours a week, 44% high-intensity care, 20% report fair or poor health attributed to caregiving; 39% say respite would help but only 13% use it (AARP/NAC 2025). Dementia caregivers provided 19.2 billion unpaid hours in 2024 valued at US$413.5 billion, about 31 hours a week each, and 59% rate emotional stress high or very high (Alzheimer's Association 2025). Pooled prevalence among informal dementia caregivers is 31% depression and 32% anxiety (UEA meta-analyses, 2020). Behavioral and psychological symptoms affect 80-90% of people with dementia; in a community sample 88.5% had clinically significant symptoms and caregivers of 79.6% reported moderate-to-severe distress (PMC9150772). A 10% rise in Neuropsychiatric Inventory score raised odds of nursing-home admission by 30% (OR 1.30). About 10-29% of community-dwelling people with dementia are prescribed antipsychotics despite the FDA boxed warning. Only 8.3% of US dementia caregivers received any training (2015-2021); under the CARE Act a UPMC study found only 22.2% of 1,801 identified caregivers received discharge training. The National Family Caregiver Support Program reaches about 700,000 a year (roughly 1%). Global dementia cost was US$1.3 trillion in 2019, projected above US$2.8 trillion by 2030 (WHO). Informal care is worth about 2.5% of EU GDP, more than public LTC spending (Eurocarers).

**Why solutions fail.** (1) Nobody in the clinical pathway is tasked with identifying the caregiver, so strain is never screened; the CARE Act (40+ states) created a duty but compliance is weak and untracked. (2) The effective interventions are known but almost never delivered: caregiver-delivered non-drug strategies reduce BPSD with moderate effects (Brodaty & Arasaratnam; Meng et al. 2021) and are guideline first-line, but REACH II, Savvy Caregiver, Tailored Activity Program and DICE are multi-session, staff-delivered and reach a tiny fraction of families. (3) Self-guided online psychoeducation fails on adherence: WHO's iSupport showed no benefit on depression or distress in the 352-carer UK RCT (Lancet Regional Health Europe, Dec 2024) because participants barely used it; the process evaluation calls for blended delivery with regular professional contact. (4) One in three clinicians believes nothing can be done about dementia (ADI 2021) and most families have no diagnosis, hence no entry point; helplines are reactive and lose the pattern between calls. (5) The US GUIDE model (July 2024; 330 organizations by July 2025) pays navigators up to US$2,563 per patient-year for respite plus care management, but navigators lack a tool that turns family observations into a tested behavior plan. (6) Existing software serves the wrong buyer or user: employer concierge (Wellthy, Cariloop) reaches only insured employees; consumer apps (ianacare, Carely, Jointly) rely on the exhausted carer to set them up; SDOH platforms (Unite Us, findhelp) centre the patient, not the carer; most apps are reminders, content libraries, forums or chatbots. (7) Entitlements (UK Carer's Assessment, Medicaid waiver respite, VA, Carer Gateway) are fragmented and hard to navigate while caring 40 hours a week.

**Intervention.** A caregiver identification-to-support loop with a built-in behavior experiment engine. At a clinical touchpoint (dementia diagnosis, hospital discharge, GP review, GUIDE enrolment) or by self-registration, a caregiver record is created (CARE Act compliance built in), the carer completes a 2-minute strain screen (Zarit-4 or Caregiver Strain Index), and the system matches them to a maintained, jurisdiction-specific catalogue of concrete supports with eligibility rules (respite grants, carer allowances and assessments, training, counselling, Medicaid waiver, VA, Carer Gateway), generates referrals and tracks whether each was received. For dementia dyads the core daily tool is a DICE-based (Describe, Investigate, Create, Evaluate) behavior-episode log: under 30 seconds per episode (what happened, time, antecedent, what helped, severity, carer distress). It aggregates episodes into time-of-day and trigger patterns, forces a physical-cause check (pain, infection, constipation, dehydration, sensory loss, new anticholinergic or sedative medicines flagged via RxNorm/RxClass), proposes a small matched set of evidence-based non-drug strategies as a two-week N-of-1 experiment, measures whether frequency, severity and distress fell, and keeps, swaps or escalates. Brief scheduled human check-ins by a coordinator or trained peer (2, 6, 12 weeks) supply the blended element the iSupport evidence says is missing, with escalation when strain rises. A clinician-facing summary (symptom and distress scores over time, what was tried, supports received) serves GUIDE navigators, GPs, memory clinics and Admiral-nurse services. Novelty: the caregiver is a patient of record with a closed-loop referral and a measured outcome, and behavior is handled as an experiment, not a content library or chat. Rule-based; no AI required.

**Business model.** Institutional buyers with an existing cost or duty: US GUIDE participants (monthly care-management payment per beneficiary plus respite billing) and Medicare chronic care and principal-illness navigation codes; hospitals (CARE Act compliance, 30-day readmission penalties; caregiver burden is associated with higher rehospitalisation odds, though the evidence is small and imprecise); Medicare Advantage plans and ACOs (caregiver supports are allowable supplemental benefits; savings on ED visits and placement); memory clinics and home care agencies; employer caregiver benefits (60% of dementia caregivers work); UK local authorities and ICBs (carer-assessment backlogs); Australian PHNs and Carer Gateway providers; dementia charities. Pricing per site or per enrolled dyad per month; family tier free; free tier for carer charities. No data sales and no pharma funding, keeping incentives aligned with fewer episodes and fewer sedating prescriptions. Honest caveat: public-sector sales cycles are slow, and navigation cannot create respite supply where none exists, so sell on identification, training completion, strain reduction and behavior-plan coverage rather than promised respite hours.

**Outcomes.** Identification: % of discharges or diagnoses with a caregiver recorded and screened (baseline unmeasured in most systems); % of identified caregivers trained before discharge (baseline about 22%). Support: % of enrolled caregivers receiving at least one concrete support within 60 days; respite use (baseline 13-16%); statutory carer-assessment completion times. Behavior: episode frequency and severity per week per dyad over 8-12 weeks versus baseline; share of episodes with a documented physical-cause check; share of enrolled dyads with a current behavior plan. Wellbeing: change in Zarit burden, NPI-Q distress and PHQ-9 at 3 and 6 months; caregiver-reported ability to take a break; employment retention. System: new antipsychotic, benzodiazepine or anticholinergic sleep-aid starts; ED visits for behavioral crises; care-recipient 30-day readmissions and ED revisits; time to long-term-care placement in a matched cohort. Not counted: downloads, articles read, chat messages.

### Competitive landscape (crowdedness 6/10)

- **WeCareAdvisor (Gitlin & Kales, NIH/NIA-funded DICE web app).** Web app that walks a family caregiver through the DICE method (Describe, Investigate, Create, Evaluate) for a behavioral symptom, surfaces non-drug strategies, and sends a daily tip. The closest existing thing to a behavior-experiment engine for families. *Shortfall:* Caregivers used it about once a month on average, so it functions as an occasional lookup, not a continuous log. No episode-level data model, no time-of-day/trigger pattern analytics, no medication/physical-cause check against RxNorm, no scheduled human check-ins, no referral loop to respite or entitlements, no clinician-facing summary, and no institutional buyer. It is also not purchasable; the Drexel/UC Davis team could commercialize, which is the main 'someone-else-might-do-this' risk.
- **Rippl Care (incl. acquired Kinto caregiver platform).** Virtual, value-based dementia care provider: clinicians plus care navigators for patients and family caregivers, GUIDE model participant, partnership with the Alzheimer's Association Dementia Care Navigation Service. Bought Kinto (AI-assisted caregiver coaching/support) in April 2024. *Shortfall:* It is a provider that sells care, not software for the other ~330-390 GUIDE programs, hospitals or memory clinics. Caregiver support is coaching/content via Kinto; no public evidence of structured N-of-1 behavior experiments, measured referral receipt, or CARE-Act-style identification at hospital discharge. Geographically limited and dependent on GUIDE/MA contracting.
- **Ceresti Health.** Caregiver empowerment program delivered on a pre-configured cellular tablet with a dedicated care navigator; sold to Medicare Advantage/D-SNP plans (e.g., PacificSource) and operates as a GUIDE participant with a 'turnkey GUIDE' offer for fee-for-service providers. *Shortfall:* Hardware (tablet) delivery raises unit cost and limits scale; evidence is company-published and small-n; plan-bought so unavailable to the uninsured, Medicaid-only or international families; no open behavior-episode data model or clinician summary that other providers can consume.
- **TCARE.** Caregiver assessment and care-planning analytics (identity-discrepancy based 'TCARE protocol') used by case managers at Area Agencies on Aging, state Medicaid programs and MCOs to produce a care plan and resource referrals. *Shortfall:* Periodic assessment by a professional, not a daily tool; condition-agnostic (no BPSD episode logging, no DICE, no medication flags); referrals are generated but receipt tracking is opaque; sold to Medicaid/AAA infrastructure so dementia families outside those programs never see it; not triggered at hospital discharge or diagnosis.
- **Trualta.** Online skills-based caregiver training library (videos/modules incl. dementia behaviors), licensed statewide by state units on aging (Kansas, New York), AAAs and health plans; now also certifies paid caregivers. *Shortfall:* It is a content library, exactly the modality the iSupport RCT showed fails on adherence when unaccompanied. No identification at clinical touchpoints, no strain screening with escalation, no individualized behavior plan or outcome measurement, no entitlement eligibility engine; outcomes claims are not independently validated.
- **ARCHANGELS (Caregiver Intensity Index).** 2.5-minute anonymous online 'intensity' quiz that scores caregivers green/yellow/red and crosswalks them to existing resources; deployed by states, employers, payers (e.g., Blue Cross Blue Shield of Massachusetts 'Any Care Counts' campaign). *Shortfall:* Deliberately collects no PII, so it cannot create a caregiver record, generate a referral, confirm receipt, or re-screen over time. Stops at awareness; nothing for behavior management; no clinical integration.
- **Careforth (formerly Seniorlink) Structured Family Caregiving.** Medicaid-waiver-funded program paying live-in family caregivers a daily stipend ($77.54-$99.71/day in Indiana) plus a care coach; recruits through referral partners. *Shortfall:* Only for Medicaid-waiver-eligible, co-resident caregivers in participating states; it is a payment and coaching service with physical operations, not a behavior-plan or identification tool; it cannot serve the majority of dementia caregivers (not Medicaid-eligible or not co-resident).
- **Carallel.** Caregiver support benefit for Medicare Advantage, D-SNP and Medicaid plans: care advocates by phone/chat plus the 'Carallel Companion' app; pitched to plans as a Stars-rating lever. *Shortfall:* Plan-member-only access; generic across conditions; advocate model is reactive (member must call); no dementia episode log, no experiment loop, no clinical identification or CARE Act hook, no clinician summary. Eligibility ≠ engagement.
- **ianacare (and employer-channel peers Wellthy, Cariloop).** Employer- and plan-sponsored caregiver navigation: care coordinators, task/help-circle app, benefit navigation; recently partnered with AARP Care Connect and Care Dimensions (a GUIDE hospice). *Shortfall:* Reaches only insured employees of client firms; relies on the exhausted caregiver to set up a help circle; coordinators handle logistics, not BPSD; no measured behavior outcomes; no CARE Act or diagnosis-triggered enrollment.
- **WHO iSupport (UK RCT) and other self-guided psychoeducation.** WHO's 23-lesson online self-help program for dementia carers, adapted in many countries. *Shortfall:* Process evaluation: 8.3% never opened it, 54% spent 30-90 minutes total; authors call for blended delivery with regular professional contact. Confirms that content-only delivery is not the answer.
- **Mobilise (UK).** Council-commissioned digital carer platform: guides, Carer's Allowance eligibility tool, online 'cuppa' peer sessions, one-to-one support; increasingly replaces local carer charities (Buckinghamshire contract from July 2026). *Shortfall:* Reach is views, not dyad outcomes; no clinical identification (GP/memory clinic/hospital), no dementia behavior tool, no measurement of strain over time or whether statutory Carer's Assessments were completed; UK-only and council-procurement dependent.
- **Care Ecosystem (UCSF) / GUIDE care-navigator programs with generic care-management software (e.g., ThoroughCare).** Telephone/internet collaborative dementia care with lay care-team navigators; the template for Medicare's GUIDE model. GUIDE participants typically run it on general care-management platforms (ThoroughCare markets GUIDE workflows: HRSN assessment, care plan, medication adherence, referrals). *Shortfall:* Navigators work from phone notes and generic care-plan templates; no standardized family-facing behavior log, no N-of-1 evaluation, no automated physical-cause/medication check, and caregiver strain is re-screened only at scheduled calls. Benefits concentrated in moderate-severe dementia with depressed caregivers, i.e., exactly where a behavior tool is needed. Funding historically grant/philanthropy dependent.
- **Dementia Support Australia (DBMAS/SBRT) and UNSW CHeBA BPSD Guide / CareForDementia apps.** National staff-delivered behavior consultancy (DSA) and free reference apps summarizing the Clinician's BPSD Guide 2023 and a carer guide. *Shortfall:* DSA is consultant-delivered and skewed to residential care, not home dyads; apps are static reference content with no logging, pattern detection or outcome tracking; Admiral Nurses (UK, ~434-455 nurses) show the same capacity ceiling for human-only models.
- **Alzheimer's Association ALZNavigator, 24/7 Helpline, Brain CareNotes (IU, I-CARE 2 trial).** ALZNavigator: free online action-plan generator; Helpline: reactive phone support; Brain CareNotes: research app with caregiver/BPSD self-monitoring and a care coach, 12-month RCT recruiting (160-184 dyads). *Shortfall:* ALZNavigator is static and unlinked to any record; the helpline loses the pattern between calls; Brain CareNotes is tied to the IU care-coach model and is years from availability.

**What remains unsolved.** Three gaps persist across every solution found. (1) Identification-to-receipt loop: nothing closes the loop from a clinical touchpoint to a confirmed support. A 2025 hospital study found only 111 of 1,698 dementia inpatient charts named a caregiver; ARCHANGELS screens anonymously and cannot refer; TCARE/Carallel/ianacare refer but do not verify receipt; Mobilise counts page views. (2) Behavior as a measured experiment: the only RCT-tested DICE tool (WeCareAdvisor) is an uncommercialized research app used about once a month with small-to-moderate effects; no shipping product offers sub-30-second episode logging, time-of-day/trigger aggregation, a forced physical-cause and anticholinergic/sedative medication check via RxNorm/RxClass, a two-week N-of-1 strategy trial with kept/swapped/escalated decisions, and a clinician summary. GUIDE's 330-390 programs run navigators on generic care-management software (ThoroughCare-style) with no behavior engine. (3) Adherence: content-only delivery fails (iSupport null; Trualta unvalidated), and the blended alternative exists only inside provider companies (Rippl, Ceresti, Care Ecosystem) that employ navigators rather than tooling third parties. Also unsolved on the data side: there is no open, machine-readable catalogue of caregiver entitlements and respite with eligibility rules; the 211 national API is in preview and restricted to contributing 211s, the Eldercare Locator API requires credentials, and Medicaid waiver respite rules live in PDF appendices.

**Differentiated wedge.** Be the behavior-experiment and caregiver-outcome layer for GUIDE dementia care programs, not another caregiver app. Concretely: a navigator-side workbench plus a lightweight family capture app, sold per enrolled dyad to GUIDE participants (public list) and memory clinics, that (a) creates the caregiver record and strain screen at enrollment/discharge (CARE Act fields built in), (b) turns the family's sub-30-second episode logs into a DICE plan with a mandatory physical-cause and RxNorm/RxClass medication check, (c) runs each strategy as a two-week N-of-1 with frequency/severity/distress deltas and keep/swap/escalate rules, (d) schedules the 2/6/12-week human check-ins that the iSupport evidence says are required, and (e) tracks every respite/entitlement referral to confirmed receipt and exports a one-page clinician summary (PDF; FHIR later) that satisfies GUIDE's care-plan and caregiver-assessment documentation. Nobody in the field holds (b)+(c)+(e) together; providers like Rippl and Ceresti are potential customers or channel partners rather than direct competitors, and content incumbents (Trualta, ALZNavigator, BPSD Guide) can be referenced inside the plan. Start US-only with one or two states dense in GUIDE participants, seed the catalogue from Eldercare Locator, state Medicaid waiver respite rules, VA Caregiver Support and ADRC listings, and expand to the UK (Admiral Nurses, SACE benchmarks, Open Referral UK feeds) once the loop is proven.

## Severely wasted children go untreated because RUTF pipelines break without warning and case-finding misses them

*Id:* `severe-wasting-rutf-pipeline` · *Merged from:* Severely wasted children go untreated because RUTF pipelines break and case-finding misses; Two in three children with severe wasting are never treated, and the therapeutic-food (RUT

**Who.** Children under 5 with severe wasting and their caregivers (mostly rural mothers walking to community clinics) in food-insecure and conflict-affected districts: northern Nigeria, Somalia, South Sudan, Sudan, Ethiopia, Kenya's arid counties, Afghanistan, Yemen, the Sahel (Niger, Senegal), plus the large caseloads in Southern Asia (India, Pakistan, Bangladesh), which holds over three-quarters of severely wasted children but where treatment coverage is lowest (Pakistan and Nepal under 5% in UNICEF-linked reporting). National wasting prevalence is 11.6% in Nigeria and 10.9% in Niger (World Bank WDI). Also the frontline workers who run outpatient therapeutic programmes (OTPs): community health workers screening with MUAC tapes (in Senegal unpaid and halved in number after 2025 US aid cuts), nutrition nurses at primary health centres, and district nutrition officers and NGO logisticians (Helen Keller Intl, ALIMA, Action Against Hunger, MSF, Save the Children) who must decide where scarce Ready-to-Use Therapeutic Food (RUTF) goes.

**Scale.** UNICEF/WHO/World Bank Joint Child Malnutrition Estimates 2025 (2024 data): 42.8 million children under 5 wasted (6.6%), of whom 12.2 million (1.9%) severely wasted. Severe wasting carries about 11.6 times the mortality of a well-nourished child (Olofin et al. 2013, 10 cohorts, 53,809 children). Only about 1 in 3 severely wasted children receives treatment (UNICEF 'No Time to Waste' 2022; Global Action Plan on Child Wasting 2020); a review of 44 coverage assessments in 21 countries found mean CMAM coverage of 38.3%, likely overestimated (Rogers et al. 2015); baseline coverage was ~1% in Gombe State, Nigeria (GiveWell 2024); treated children relapse at 22-63% within six months (King et al. 2025). UNICEF RUTF Market and Supply Update (Dec 2025): 86,500 MT delivered in 2024, enough for ~6.2 million children against 12.2 million in need; funded demand ~80,000 MT/year versus 290,000 MT offered capacity, so the binding constraint is funding and planning, not manufacturing. 2025 pipeline crisis: Save the Children (Aug 2025) warned Nigeria, Kenya, Somalia and South Sudan could run out of RUTF within ~3 months; only 39% of 2025 nutrition funding received; Nigeria secured 64% of 629,000 cartons needed; South Sudan treated only one third of SAM children Jan-Jul 2025 after 15% of facilities closed. UNICEF 2024 appeal: nearly 2 million severely malnourished children at risk of death from RUTF funding shortfalls. 2026 sitreps: Sudan RUTF pipeline gap ~US$21.2M, facility stock-out rate 11%; Afghanistan pipeline interruptions since April 2026 cut admissions. NPR (June 2026): screenings in one Senegalese district fell from 180,000+ (Oct-Dec 2024) to under 87,000 (Jul-Sep 2025); Diourbel warehouse receives about half its former RUTF.

**Why solutions fail.** CMAM with RUTF is a proven, standardized treatment, so the failure is in reach and continuity, not efficacy. (1) Funding is volatile and short-cycle; UNICEF's 2025 update says funding 'remains unpredictable and insufficient' and lists forecasting among the main supply challenges; 2025 cuts removed CHW staff and product at the same time. (2) Commodity planning uses national annual consumption/morbidity methods (Quantimed/PipeLine, NutriDash annual reporting) from static prevalence, which cannot anticipate district-level surges driven by drought, floods, price spikes, displacement or lean seasons; stock-outs then cause defaulting and deaths. (3) Supply-chain systems (OpenLMIS, DHIS2 logistics modules) are backward-looking monthly facility reports that record a stockout after it happens; early-warning data (IPC, FEWS NET, CHIRPS, WFP prices) and treatment data (OTP admissions, LMIS stock) sit in separate silos. Action Against Hunger's MERIAM (since 2017, 6-8 months lead time) remains a research pilot with no shared operational tool. (4) Coverage assessments (SQUEAC, 21+ countries) are expensive one-off surveys; they consistently find distance, opportunity cost and lack of awareness as dominant barriers, and relapse is unmonitored. (5) Simplified, lower-dose protocols (ComPAS, OptiMA) can cut RUTF per recovered child by 32-50% (Bailey et al. 2020, Daures et al. 2025) but planners lack tools to model their stock implications. (6) Leakage and black-market diversion of RUTF (documented in Nigeria) worsen shortages, and under scarcity there is no principled way to allocate cartons across districts by expected caseload and mortality risk.

**Intervention.** An open, district- and facility-level RUTF pipeline early-warning and allocation platform for MoH district nutrition officers, UNICEF/WFP offices and Nutrition Cluster NGOs. It ingests (a) monthly or weekly OTP admissions, discharges and stock-on-hand from DHIS2 (Web API) or CSV/Excel uploads (offline-friendly), and (b) public leading indicators: IPC/CH acute food insecurity phases (IPC public API, WFP DataBridges), FEWS NET outlooks, CHIRPS/Open-Meteo rainfall anomalies, WFP/FAO market prices and ACLED conflict events via HDX, seasonal lean-period calendars, screening coverage and WorldPop under-5 population. It produces per-district 1-3 month admissions forecasts using transparent seasonal baselines adjusted by leading indicators (back-tested error bands, no LLM required), converts forecasts into cartons required under standard vs simplified (ComPAS/OptiMA) protocols, computes weeks-of-stock and projected stock-out dates against in-country pipeline deliveries, raises pipeline-break alerts 4-8 weeks ahead, and recommends reorders and cross-district reallocations under a stated priority rule (predicted caseload x severity). A lightweight family-MUAC follow-up register catches relapse after discharge. Shared views replace siloed spreadsheets with one agreed picture. Novel because no operational tool links anticipatory early warning to commodity planning at district granularity, forecasting today is national and annual, simplified-protocol scenarios are not modelled, and allocation under scarcity, the central problem after the 2025 funding collapse, is ad hoc.

**Business model.** Not a caregiver-paying product. Revenue comes from organizations that already procure nutrition and supply-chain information systems: (1) hosted, supported tier sold per district/country to UNICEF country offices, WFP and Nutrition Cluster INGOs (Save the Children, Action Against Hunger, IRC, ALIMA) covering hosting, integration and forecast calibration; (2) MoH procurement financed through World Bank, Global Financing Facility and nutrition supply-chain strengthening lines; (3) analytics subscriptions for RUTF manufacturers and procurement agencies that need demand forecasts; (4) an open-core 'global good' model (open-source core, data owned by the ministry, paid hosting/SLA), consistent with Digital Square's 2025 roadmap naming government ownership and structured market pathways as sustainability gaps, with philanthropic funding for the public-good layer. Honest caveats: payers' budgets are shrinking after the 2025 funding collapse and revenue depends on the same aid system that is contracting; a small team is viable on roughly 5-10 country contracts, and keeping the core and forecast data open preserves social value if a customer lapses.

**Outcomes.** District-months and facility stockout-days with RUTF stock-outs before vs after adoption, and versus matched districts; share of OTP sites reporting at least 8 weeks of stock; share of admitted children whose treatment was interrupted by stockouts; forecast accuracy (MAPE of monthly/weekly admissions vs actuals, by lead time); lead time of pipeline-break warnings that proved correct and days between signal and confirmed reorder or reallocation; treated/needed ratio (coverage) and screening coverage (children screened / target population) in participating districts; cure, defaulter and death rates per Sphere standards in OTP registers; relapse at 6 months among followed-up discharges; RUTF cartons per recovered child; cartons expired or unaccounted for; admissions-to-stock ratio during lean seasons.

- **UNICEF NutriDash + RUTF Market Dashboard + Humanitarian Nutrition Commodity Funding Updates.** UNICEF's global nutrition programme-data platform (annual country reporting on CMAM admissions, coverage and supply) explicitly aims to support supply forecasting; the RUTF Market Dashboard publishes delivery/market data; quarterly Humanitarian Nutrition Commodity Funding Updates (Q2 2026) track RUTF pipeline/stock-out risk across 27 priority contexts and the 9.4M-carton 2026-27 target (7.8M secured). *Shortfall:* Annual, national and retrospective: country data are only shown publicly where governments consent, there is no district/facility granularity, no projected stock-out dates, no link to leading indicators (IPC, rainfall, prices, conflict), and no allocation logic. **What remains unsolved.** Nobody operates a tool that converts a district's admissions trend plus public leading indicators into (a) a 1-3 month admissions forecast, (b) cartons required under standard vs simplified protocols, (c) a projected stock-out date against known in-country deliveries, and (d) a ranked reallocation/reorder recommendation. ## Zero-dose and dropout children are invisible because facility denominators and defaulter tracking are broken

*Id:* `zero-dose-immunization-tracing` · *Merged from:* Under-vaccinated children are invisible to immunization programmes because denominators an

**Who.** Infants and toddlers who never start or do not complete the routine schedule, concentrated in three settings Gavi identifies as holding roughly half of all zero-dose children: urban slums, remote rural communities and conflict-affected areas. Largest absolute numbers are in Nigeria (WorldPop estimates ~2.1 million zero-dose in 2024), DR Congo, Ethiopia, India (679,000 in 2025, down from 909,000), Pakistan, Indonesia, Yemen, Sudan and Afghanistan; more than half of zero-dose children live in fragile and conflict-affected settings that hold only about a third of the world's children. Also the frontline: facility nurses keeping paper tally sheets and tickler files, community health workers (CHWs) asked to trace defaulters without lists, and district EPI managers planning outreach with target populations they know are wrong.

**Scale.** WHO/UNICEF WUENIC estimates: 14.3 million zero-dose children in 2024 (released July 2025) and 13.5 million in 2025 (released July 2026), still above the 2019 baseline of 12.9 million and about 4 million above the Immunization Agenda 2030 trajectory. Nearly 20 million infants missed at least one DTP dose in 2024; 7.3 million infants received DTP1 but dropped out before their first measles dose in 2025. Coverage in 2025: DTP1 90%, DTP3 85%, MCV1 84%, MCV2 77%, all below the 95% needed for measles control; 57 countries reported large or disruptive measles outbreaks in 2025 and WHO estimated ~30 million children at risk for measles (2024 release). Zero-dose children in fragile/conflict settings rose from 3.6 million (2019) to 5.4 million (2024). Data-quality evidence: a PLoS ONE analysis of WHO-reported data 2000-2016 found administrative coverage above 100% in 11% of BCG and 6% of DTP3 reporting events; 13 South African districts logged >100% coverage in 2021; an Ethiopian study found DHIS2 underestimated pentavalent dropout versus DHS, with negative dropout in 3.3% of zone-years. Note: primary pages were read via domain-restricted search excerpts because direct fetches were blocked.

**Why solutions fail.** (1) Administrative coverage divides doses by projected target populations that are frequently wrong, so programmes cannot tell a well-covered facility from one whose denominator is too small; WHO denominator guidance recommends triangulation but it is rarely operationalized monthly. (2) Household surveys (DHS/MICS) come every 3-5 years and, as Gavi-linked reviewers note, under-represent the very children most likely to be missed. (3) Electronic immunization registries are expensive, partial and often unmaintained; most facilities still use paper registers and tickler boxes, so defaulter lists are built by hand. (4) Reminder and tracing work when individual data exist (Iraq DHIS2 registry + SMS: pentavalent dropout 5.7% vs 29.7% control; Kenya phone tracing cut dropout below 10% in 11 of 12 facilities; South Sudan tickler files 18% to 11%), but a Ugandan cluster RCT of app-based community defaulter tracing improved only DPT3-MCV dropout and required extra manpower: tools that add work without lists and denominators fail. (5) High-resolution zero-dose maps (WorldPop/GRID3 Bayesian 1 km models, ADM2 CSVs for Nigeria, DRC, Cameroon, Chad, Mali) are published as research datasets, explicitly "not official statistics", and not wired into monthly microplanning. (6) The DHIS2 Immunization Analysis App computes dropout using the official target population and does not reconcile denominators spatially or generate outreach or defaulter workflows. (7) Conflict, insecurity, supply disruption and misinformation compound all of the above.

**Intervention.** A DHIS2-native facility catchment denominator-reconciliation and dropout-tracing web tool. (1) It builds facility catchments from open master facility lists (MoH lists, healthsites.io/OSM) and gridded under-1 population (WorldPop, GRID3 settlements) with travel-time surfaces, producing an alternative expected birth cohort per facility with uncertainty, and flags facilities whose official target population is inconsistent with DTP1/BCG counts. (2) It pulls monthly antigen doses from the DHIS2 aggregate API to compute per-facility dropout (DTP1 to DTP3, DTP1 to MCV1, MCV1 to MCV2) and estimated zero-dose counts, ranks facilities and settlements for outreach, and produces a printable monthly microplan. (3) Where a DHIS2 Tracker or other child registry exists, it generates weekly due/overdue lists per CHW, sends scheduled SMS reminders through local gateways, and records tracing outcomes so the loop closes. Novelty: it operationalizes geospatial denominators (currently research-only) into routine monthly planning and ties them to defaulter lists, which neither the DHIS2 immunization app nor standalone registries do. It is useful with no AI at all; the value is denominator triangulation, dropout arithmetic and short, prioritized worklists.

**Business model.** Buyers are ministries of health and their funders rather than families. (1) Gavi Health Systems Strengthening and zero-dose funding lines explicitly finance identifying and reaching zero-dose children, and UNICEF/WHO country offices procure immunization information tools. (2) Open-core global-good model: open-source core with paid hosting, DHIS2 integration, training and SLA, which is how DHIS2 ecosystem apps are sustained. (3) Contracts with civil-society organizations implementing Gavi's zero-dose programmes. Caveats: donor budgets tightened in 2025 and the 2025 Digital Square roadmap documents that global goods struggle with inconsistent funding and weak government ownership; pricing should be modest per country (tens of thousands of dollars per year) and the core must stay open so a lapsed contract does not strand a ministry's data.

**Outcomes.** DTP1-to-DTP3 and MCV1-to-MCV2 dropout at participating facilities (target below 10%) versus matched facilities; number and share of overdue children traced and vaccinated within 30 days of due date; share of facilities whose reconciled denominator is consistent with DTP1 counts and whose reported coverage no longer exceeds 100%; zero-dose children found and started on the schedule through outreach to flagged settlements; cost per additional fully immunized child; measles cases in catchment areas over 2-3 years; CHW time per traced child.

### Competitive landscape (crowdedness 6/10)

- **DHIS2 / WHO Immunization Analysis App (dhis2/who-immunization-analysis-app).** WHO-copyrighted (2017), GPL-3 DHIS2 app (package.json version 1.0.8, AngularJS 1.7 + chart.js) that renders coverage, 'performance: coverage vs dropout' (DPT3 coverage vs DPT1-DPT3 dropout, 90%/10% quadrants), data-quality consistency and monitoring charts from DHIS2 aggregate data. Each antigen is mapped to a 'vaccine (total)', 'vaccine (target age)' and a 'denominator' data element/indicator supplied by the country. *Shortfall:* It is a viewer: dropout = doses arithmetic and coverage = doses / whatever target population the ministry typed in. It never questions the denominator, has no spatial reconciliation, no estimate of zero-dose children per facility, no ranking of facilities/settlements, no printable microplan and no defaulter/due lists. A facility with a too-small denominator simply shows >100% coverage.
- **DHIS2 WHO immunization metadata packages: EPI Aggregate (IMM_AGG), Triangulation Dashboard (IMM_TRI), Immunization eRegistry Tracker (IMM_EIR), Big Catch-Up (IMM_BCU), Demand Module (IMM_DM).** Configuration packages (data sets, indicators, dashboards, tracker program) maintained by UiO/HISP with WHO, CDC, UNICEF and Gavi funding. **What remains unsolved.** Nobody produces a defensible, facility-level expected birth cohort that a district EPI manager can see next to last month's DTP1/DTP3/MCV1 counts. The WHO/DHIS2 packages admit denominators are 'a difficult task' and leave them as annually keyed numbers; the triangulation dashboard surfaces negative dropout and >100% coverage but cannot say which of the numerator or denominator is wrong; WorldPop/GRID3 have the geospatial answer but publish it as non-official research layers keyed to grid cells and admin units, not to DHIS2 org units, and on a multi-year cadence; registries, CHW apps and biometrics only act on children who are already enrolled, which excludes exactly the zero-dose children in slums, remote hamlets and conflict areas. Three concrete gaps remain: (1) catchment construction and under-1 denominator estimation with uncertainty per DHIS2 facility org unit, refreshed monthly and reconciled against DTP1/BCG-implied cohorts; (2) turning the reconciled gap (expected minus vaccinated) into a ranked list of settlements and facilities for outreach and a printable monthly microplan; (3) closing the loop to Tracker due/overdue lists and SMS only where child-level data exist, without adding CHW workload. Harder, unsolved-by-anyone problems that a software MVP cannot fully fix: ministry willingness to display 'coverage above 100%' and revise official targets, data-sharing agreements for facility-level DHIS2 access, SMS cost and connectivity, and conflict-zone field access.

**Differentiated wedge.** A DHIS2 App Hub-installable 'Denominator Reconciliation and Outreach Prioritisation' app that works on aggregate data only, so it is useful at the 80%+ of facilities still on paper. Wedge sequence: (1) For each facility org unit, pull 24 months of BCG/DTP1/DTP3/MCV1/MCV2 from /api/analytics and the official target population; build catchments from org-unit geometry plus healthsites/OSM points and MAP travel-time surfaces; query WOPR polyagesex for under-1 population with credible intervals; produce three denominators (official, DTP1/BCG-implied, geospatial) and a one-line verdict per facility ('official target likely 35% too low', 'DTP1 exceeds any plausible cohort: check tally sheets'). (2) Compute dropout and an estimated unreached-under-1 count with uncertainty, rank facilities and GRID3 settlements by expected unvaccinated children per outreach session, and generate a printable monthly microplan plus a DHIS2 data set of 'reconciled target' for the ministry to adopt or reject. (3) Only where IMM_EIR Tracker exists, generate per-CHW due/overdue lists via the Tracker API, schedule SMS through the existing DHIS2 gateway, and write back tracing outcomes. Narrow first market: Nigerian states (richest open inputs: WorldPop 1 km zero-dose 2000-2024, GRID3 settlements, NPHCDA HFR) sold through Gavi/UNICEF-funded state immunization programmes and CSO zero-dose implementers; open-core with paid hosting, integration and SLA. Deliberately no AI, no custom hardware, no child-level data in the core, and all outputs expressed as ranges to survive the politics of >100% coverage.

## Mothers get no structured risk-stratified follow-up in the postpartum weeks when most maternal deaths occur

*Id:* `postpartum-followup-gap` · *Merged from:* Mothers fall through the gap after birth: no structured follow-up in the days and weeks wh

**Who.** Women in the first six weeks to twelve months after birth, especially those discharged within 24 hours of a facility delivery with unrecognized risk (hypertensive disorders, haemorrhage, caesarean, anaemia, infection, depression). In low- and lower-middle-income countries, which account for 92% of the 260,000 maternal deaths (2023), roughly a third of women receive no postnatal check in the first days after birth; sub-Saharan Africa carries 70% of deaths, and newborn postnatal-care coverage ranges from 97% (South Africa) to 43% (Ethiopia) in recent DHS analyses. In the United States: women in the 35.1% of counties that are maternity care deserts (2.3 million women of reproductive age, 38-minute average travel to a birthing hospital, more than 100 obstetric units closed since 2022), Medicaid-insured and Black women, and women with postpartum hypertension or perinatal mental illness. Also midwives, nurses and community health workers (CHWs) who have no list of who was discharged yesterday with which risks.

**Scale.** WHO/UN: 260,000 maternal deaths in 2023 (712 per day), MMR 197 per 100,000, progress stalled since 2016. WHO analysis in The Lancet Global Health (2024/25, deaths 2009-2020): haemorrhage 27% and hypertensive disorders 16% of maternal deaths; most haemorrhage and sepsis deaths occur postpartum, and rising facility birth has not reduced them. WHO (March 2025): about a third of women, mostly in lower-income countries, miss essential postnatal checks in the first days after birth. UNICEF (2023): only 69% of mothers and 68% of newborns received a postnatal check within the recommended period globally. A 2022 JBI systematic review of postpartum timing: of maternal deaths within 42 days, 48.9% occur on day 1, 24.5% on days 2-7 and 24.9% on days 8-42; 81% of eclampsia/hypertensive deaths occur in the first week and 61% of infection deaths on days 8-42, i.e. after discharge and before the 6-week visit. Perinatal depression affects about 1 in 4 women in LMICs (JAMA Psychiatry 2023 meta-analysis, 616,708 women, 51 countries; 25.5% in lower-middle-income countries). US (CDC MMRCs): 53% of pregnancy-related deaths occurred 7 days to 1 year after pregnancy (2017-2019), 47% in the 2020 38-state dataset; more than 80% judged preventable (84% in 2020); mental health conditions the leading underlying cause (23%); roughly 70% of US hypertension-related maternal deaths occur postpartum (secondary citation).

**Why solutions fail.** (1) WHO's 2022 postnatal care recommendations specify contacts at 24 hours, day 3, days 7-14 and 6 weeks, but facilities keep no risk-stratified lists of discharged women, so the schedule is not operationalized; CHW programmes make routine visits without knowing who had a hypertensive disorder or haemorrhage. (2) The conventional 6-week visit is mis-timed: hypertensive deaths and readmissions cluster in the first week (a 2025 US series found all hypertension readmissions within 7 days of discharge), yet attendance at early postpartum clinic visits is poor (44% at Penn before its text programme). (3) The one well-evidenced model, Penn's Heart Safe Motherhood (home cuff plus twice-daily text-reported BP; readmissions for postpartum hypertension fell from 5% to 1%, 92% engagement), is single-condition, embedded in one health system's EHR and limited to a few US academic centres; home BP monitoring in LMICs is feasible (a 12-week Nigeria programme showed high recruitment, fidelity and retention) but has not been scaled. (4) Mental health screening is rare and disconnected from physical follow-up; CDC committees found 100% of pregnancy-related mental-health deaths preventable and 63% in days 43-365. (5) Health information systems (EHRs, DHIS2) effectively stop at delivery; postpartum data are not captured, so no one can measure the gap. (6) Payment: US remote-monitoring reimbursement is patchy and most Medicaid plans do not fund it; LMIC programmes depend on donors. (7) Obstetric unit closures lengthen distances, making the in-person 6-week visit even less likely in rural US areas.

**Intervention.** A risk-stratified postpartum follow-up registry and protocol engine, implemented from WHO's SMART Guidelines postnatal-care Digital Adaptation Kit (L2, July 2025) and the WHO self-monitoring-of-blood-pressure SMART guideline, on FHIR. At discharge, a midwife or nurse enrols mother and newborn with structured risk flags (hypertensive disorder, postpartum haemorrhage, caesarean, anaemia, preterm or low-birth-weight infant, HIV, adolescent, Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale score). The system generates a follow-up plan (24 h, day 3, days 7-14, 6 weeks, plus BP checks on days 3-10 for hypertensive women and EPDS re-screens at 2 and 6 weeks), creates daily worklists for CHWs and nurses, collects BP readings and coded danger-sign checklists via SMS/USSD keypad responses or CHW data entry (structured fields, not free-text chat), applies explicit thresholds (BP 140/90 or 160/110, EPDS 13 or self-harm item, fever, heavy bleeding) to route cases to a clinician queue with time-to-response tracking, and closes each episode. One core serves two markets: rural and Medicaid clinics in the US (postpartum hypertension and EPDS follow-up without a particular EHR) and MoH/CHW programmes in LMICs. Novelty: no open, multi-condition postpartum registry implements the WHO schedule end to end; existing programmes are single-condition, proprietary and US-only, and mental health is never in the same workflow as hypertension and bleeding. No AI needed; value is enrolment at discharge, protocol timing, worklists and escalation. Depends on commodity BP cuffs (about USD 15-30, shareable at CHW level), a real but not custom-hardware dependency.

**Business model.** Two complementary payers, neither of them the mother. (1) United States: health systems and Medicaid managed-care organizations pay to reduce postpartum hypertension readmissions and meet postpartum-care quality measures now that most states cover 12 months postpartum (CDC committees documented Medicaid extensions driven by their recommendations); remote physiologic monitoring CPT codes exist, so per-enrolled-patient or per-site subscriptions are realistic. (2) LMICs: ministry of health and donor procurement (UNFPA, Gates-funded MNCH programmes, World Bank/GFF maternal health projects, Jhpiego-type implementers) as an open-core global good aligned with WHO SMART Guidelines, which lowers adoption friction for ministries. Keeping the core open-source and patient-facing SMS free protects the social value; US margins can cross-subsidize LMIC deployments.

**Outcomes.** Share of enrolled women with a documented contact by day 3 and by day 14 (baseline: roughly a third miss any early check in LMICs); share of women with hypertensive disorders with at least one BP reading on days 3-10; median time from an abnormal BP or EPDS result to clinician action; postpartum hypertension readmissions within 42 days (US sites; benchmark 5% to 1% at Penn); EPDS-positive women referred and starting treatment; severe maternal morbidity, near-miss and death counts among enrolled women versus facility baseline; postnatal-care coverage in the catchment as reported to DHIS2 or DHS; CHW visits per high-risk versus low-risk woman (evidence of prioritization).

### Competitive landscape (crowdedness 6/10)

- **Heart Safe Motherhood (Penn Medicine, Nudge Unit).** Text-message postpartum hypertension programme: women with a hypertensive disorder are discharged with a home cuff and text BP readings twice daily for ~10 days; a rules engine replies and nurses act on out-of-range values. Became standard of care at Penn and is the reference evidence base cited by the opportunity (RCT: 92% BP ascertainment vs 44% with office visits; readmissions fell in pre/post data). *Shortfall:* Single condition (hypertension only); tied to Penn's EHR and nursing workflow; English/Spanish SMS to a personal phone; no risk register at discharge for other conditions (haemorrhage, sepsis, EPDS); US academic-centre context; outcome numbers come from one health system and are often quoted as if generalisable.
- **Babyscripts.** Proprietary 'virtual maternity care' platform: patient smartphone app plus connected BP cuff for prenatal and postpartum hypertension monitoring, with add-on mental-health screening and education modules; clinician dashboard; EHR integrations. *Shortfall:* Smartphone-app and Bluetooth-cuff dependent (poor fit for keypad phones, USSD, CHWs); hypertension-first with EPDS bolted on rather than one multi-condition escalation queue; proprietary and US-only; priced for health systems, not rural critical-access hospitals or MoH programmes; no CHW worklists or WHO contact schedule; outcome claims are vendor-published observational data.
- **Pomelo Care.** Virtual maternity care provider: employs its own nurses, doulas, midwives and physicians to deliver 24/7 telehealth prenatal and postpartum care (including BP and mental-health follow-up) for payer-contracted members; acquired The Doula Network in 2024. *Shortfall:* It is a care-delivery company, not software for existing facilities or CHW programmes; it substitutes for, rather than equips, the discharging hospital's nurses; US-only and dependent on payer contracts and the 12-month Medicaid postpartum extension; nothing is open or reusable by ministries of health.
- **Maven Clinic.** Employer- and health-plan-sponsored virtual women's and family health platform with postpartum support (care advocates, telehealth, mental health). *Shortfall:* Commercially insured population; a navigation and telehealth benefit, not a risk-stratified clinical follow-up protocol; does not serve Medicaid-heavy rural hospitals or LMICs; no BP/EPDS threshold escalation tied to a discharge event.
- **Jacaranda Health PROMPTS (Kenya).** Two-way SMS platform for pregnant and postpartum women in public facilities: stage-based messages, free-text questions triaged by an NLP model to a nurse helpdesk, referral of danger signs to facilities; expanding beyond Kenya. *Shortfall:* Enrolment is at antenatal visit or self-enrolment, not triggered by a discharge risk assessment; free-text + AI triage rather than structured BP readings and coded danger-sign checklists; no CHW worklists or facility-side register; mental health handled lightly; helpdesk is Jacaranda-run rather than the facility's own clinician queue; proprietary, donor-funded.
- **MomConnect (South Africa NDoH).** National registration of pregnant women at public ANC clinics with stage-based SMS/WhatsApp messages through one year postpartum, a helpdesk for questions and complaints, and feedback to the NDoH. *Shortfall:* Informational push messaging with a helpdesk; no clinical risk stratification, no BP or EPDS data capture, no escalation queue with time-to-response; evaluations show modest behavioural effects; sustainability tied to donor funding and messaging costs; not a reusable product for other ministries.
- **Community Health Toolkit (Medic) incl. Kenya eCHIS.** Open-source CHW platform (offline-first Android app + supervisor dashboards) with configurable pregnancy and postnatal-care task schedules (e.g., PNC visits at days 3, 7, 42), referrals and messaging; Kenya's national eCHIS for 100,000+ community health promoters is built on it. *Shortfall:* Starts from community registration, not from a facility discharge event, so the handoff of a high-risk discharged woman to the right CHW is manual or absent; PNC logic is per-deployment configuration, not a packaged WHO-DAK engine with BP/EPDS thresholds and clinician queues; heavy implementer configuration; in Kenya it is national infrastructure you must integrate with rather than compete against.
- **CommCare (Dimagi) and OpenSRP 2 (Ona).** General-purpose case-management toolkits for frontline workers. CommCare: form/case builder used for hundreds of MNCH projects. OpenSRP 2 (https://smartregister.org/): FHIR-native platform aligned with WHO SMART Guidelines, with a WHO ANC reference app. *Shortfall:* Toolkits, not products: every programme rebuilds PNC forms, schedules and thresholds itself; no packaged risk-stratified postnatal registry or discharge-triggered worklists; OpenSRP's PNC content lags its ANC reference app and there is no WHO PNC L3 implementation to install; cost and implementer dependence at scale.
- **WHO SMART Guidelines PNC Digital Adaptation Kit (L2) and DHIS2 RMNCAH packages.** WHO's machine-readable specification of postnatal-care personas, workflows, data dictionary, decision logic and indicators (L2, 2025), alongside DHIS2's aggregate RMNCAH metadata packages and Tracker-based mother/child registries (e.g., the NIPH/Palestinian MoH eRegistry). *Shortfall:* A specification without software: no open L3 FHIR profiles, no reference implementation, no SMS/USSD channel; DHIS2 aggregate packages report PNC coverage after the fact and Tracker deployments are heavy, facility-bound and lack time-to-response escalation; eRegistry's cluster RCT showed limited quality effects.
- **Kilkari (India MoHFW, BBC Media Action origin).** Largest maternal mobile messaging programme: weekly automated voice messages from second trimester to one year postpartum, linked to the national RCH registry. *Shortfall:* One-way, non-personalised content; rigorous evaluation (BMJ Global Health 2022) found modest effects on a few behaviours and none on most; no risk stratification, data capture or escalation.

## The days after a psychiatric discharge or self-harm ED visit are the highest-risk and least-owned part of the suicide-prevention pathway

*Id:* `post-psychiatric-discharge-safety-bridge` · *Merged from:* The first days after a psychiatric discharge or a self-harm emergency visit: the highest-r

**Who.** People leaving psychiatric inpatient units or emergency departments after a suicide attempt or self-harm, and their families. US: ~616,000 ED visits a year for self-harm injury (CDC FastStats); CDC MMWR (June 2026) identified 833,335 suspected-attempt ED visits in the NSSP sample 2021-2025 (15.8 per 10,000 ED visits), highest among girls 12-17 and rising among adults 26+ and 65+. England: roughly 70-75k eligible adult acute discharges a year (RCPsych: 160,430 eligible discharges Apr 2020-May 2022). Globally, 73% of the 727,000 suicides in 2021 occurred in low- and middle-income countries (WHO), where structured aftercare barely exists. The population skews to young adults (suicide is the 3rd leading cause of death at 15-29, WHO) and to people with depression, substance use and recent crises.

**Scale.** WHO: 727,000 suicide deaths in 2021 (likely an undercount), 3rd leading cause of death at ages 15-29; US: 48,824 suicide deaths in 2024 (CDC). Post-discharge risk is extreme and concentrated: pooled suicide rate in the first week after psychiatric discharge is ~2,950 per 100,000 person-years (95% CI 1,740-5,000; 24 studies; Chung et al., BMJ Open 2019) and ~1,132 per 100,000 in the first 3 months (Chung et al., JAMA Psychiatry 2017), versus ~9-14 per 100,000 in the general population, i.e. roughly 100x. UK NCISH (Feb 2025 report, data 2012-2022): ~198 patient suicides in 2022 occurred within 3 months of discharge (11% of all patient suicides), the number and rate have risen since 2017, and deaths peak on days 3-6 after discharge. Follow-up performance is poor on both sides of the Atlantic: NCQA HEDIS 7-day follow-up after mental-illness hospitalization averaged 38.5% nationally in MY2023 (36.6% in 2022); NHS England's 72-hour follow-up standard (80% target) was met for only 73% of eligible adults as of April 2023 (Nuffield Trust) and 76% across Apr 2020-May 2022 (RCPsych).

**Why solutions fail.** (1) Nobody owns the gap: inpatient responsibility ends at discharge and the outpatient appointment is often 2-4 weeks away, so peak-risk days fall between two systems. (2) Follow-up is unfunded staff time: a 2023 Zero Suicide ED evaluation found "limited resources needed to engage and re-engage at-risk patients ... and provide follow-up supportive contacts during transitions"; UCHealth cited leadership buy-in, resources and funding as barriers. (3) Evidence-based pieces exist but are rarely delivered together: Safety Planning Intervention plus structured phone follow-up within 72h roughly halved suicidal behaviour at 6 months in VA EDs (3.03% vs 5.29%, Stanley et al., JAMA Psychiatry 2018) and doubled treatment attendance; caring contacts have mixed evidence (Comtois 2019 RCT: null primary outcome, fewer attempts as a secondary outcome, 9% vs 15%). Implementations are bespoke and home-grown (Nationwide Children's EHR-automated texts, VA Caring Letters); no dominant specialised vendor surfaced. (4) Consumer safety-plan apps (Stay Alive, Beyond Now, MY3) are not connected to the discharging team and cannot tell anyone whether the 7-day appointment happened. (5) Non-clinical aftercare without strong linkage underperforms: Australia's Way Back Support Service showed no reduction in repeat self-poisoning admissions (McGill 2022); the national evaluation (Nous 2022) said referral effectiveness "was constrained by the quality of those services".

**Intervention.** A hospital- and ED-deployable "discharge-to-first-appointment safety bridge": a multi-tenant web platform that, at discharge, (a) digitises the Stanley-Brown safety plan and lets the patient share it with one or two chosen support people, who get their own simple view and a short role script; (b) schedules a protocolised, human-signed, non-demanding caring-contact series (SMS/email on days 1, 3, 7, 14, 30, then monthly to 12 months, mirroring trial cadences); (c) tracks the two hard targets payers and regulators already measure, contact within 72 hours and a mental-health appointment within 7/30 days, and raises a worklist alert to the discharging clinic when either has not happened; (d) routes any reply matching an escalation rule to an on-call human and the national crisis line; and (e) emits HEDIS FUH / NHS MHS80-style metrics. What is novel is the closed loop (did follow-up actually occur, and who is accountable today) plus the support-person channel, packaged so a mid-sized hospital can adopt it in weeks rather than as a research project. It is not a chatbot: AI is optional (e.g. triaging free-text replies) and rules-based keyword escalation suffices for an MVP; the value is in scheduling, accountability and measurement.

**Business model.** Sell to institutions, never to patients. US: Medicaid managed-care plans and Medicare Advantage are scored and paid on HEDIS FUH, hospitals face Joint Commission suicide-prevention requirements, and 30-day readmissions are penalised, so a per-discharge or per-site SaaS fee is justifiable from existing budgets. UK: trusts are contractually measured on 72-hour follow-up. Australia: aftercare is federally commissioned. Pricing per enrolled discharge (tens of dollars) against a readmission costing thousands keeps the social value intact; an open-source core with paid hosting/compliance would let LMIC NGOs use it free.

**Outcomes.** % of discharges with a documented contact within 72 hours (baseline ~73% England, lower elsewhere); % with a mental-health appointment within 7 and 30 days (baseline 38.5% / ~60% US); repeat self-harm ED visits and psychiatric readmissions at 30, 90 and 180 days; suicidal behaviour at 6 months (trial benchmark: 3.0% vs 5.3%); safety-plan access events and support-person activation; time-to-escalation for flagged replies; and, with registry linkage, suicide deaths within 90 days of discharge.

## Lay-counsellor mental health care in LMICs cannot be supervised, measured or kept to fidelity at scale

*Id:* `task-shared-mental-health-quality` · *Merged from:* Depression and anxiety go untreated in low- and middle-income countries because the only s

**Who.** Adults and adolescents with depression, anxiety and related distress in low- and lower-middle-income countries (sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, humanitarian settings), where specialist care is effectively absent outside capital cities. Equally, the non-specialist workforce asked to fill the gap: community health workers, 'grandmother' counsellors (Friendship Bench in Zimbabwe, Malawi, Kenya, Vietnam, Jordan), lay facilitators (StrongMinds Uganda/Zambia), PM+ helpers in refugee settings, and the small number of psychologists expected to supervise them. Downstream: NGO program managers and ministry officials who must show funders that this care works.

**Scale.** WHO (World Mental Health Today, Sept 2025; 2021 data): more than 1 billion people live with a mental disorder; 71% of people with psychosis receive no services; governments spend a median 2.1% of health budgets on mental health, unchanged since 2017 (Mental Health Atlas 2024). Workforce: global median 13.5 specialised mental-health workers per 100,000, but about 1 per 100,000 in low-income countries versus 60+ in high-income countries. Minimally adequate treatment for major depression ranged from 23% in high-income to 3% in low- and lower-middle-income countries (Moitra et al., PLOS Medicine 2022; 84 countries, 2000-2019). 73% of the world's 727,000 suicides (2021) occur in LMICs. Task-sharing works but scale is fragile: Friendship Bench reports 800k-1M clients since 2016 and 5,000+ trained counsellors in Zimbabwe; a 2026 systematic review of Friendship Bench implementation across Africa lists 'limited supervision' and 'inadequate incentives' as barriers and finds cost-effectiveness under enhanced supervision but not under basic internal-champion models. The 2025 US aid cuts removed services for ~750,000 people and training for ~50,000 prospective mental-health workers across 131 programs in 32 countries (GMHAN/MHIN survey), on top of a public financing gap estimated at US$200bn+.

**Why solutions fail.** The binding constraint on task-shared psychological care is not training but what happens after it: sustained supervision, fidelity monitoring, outcome measurement and safe escalation. Reviews of non-specialist-delivered interventions in LMICs repeatedly note that 'empirical evaluations of supervision are scarce' (perinatal review), that only 9 youth-intervention studies provided ongoing supervision, and that 'sustainable models of training and supervision' are the stated next step. WHO/UNICEF's EQUIP platform (2022) solved competency assessment at the point of training (794 training programmes, 36 countries, 3,760 trainees, 10,001 assessments by March 2024) but stops at training; it does not run the service. Harvard/Sangath's EMPOWER adds digital training and peer supervision but remains at pilot scale (e.g., 40+ CHWs in Gujarat) and India-focused. Most programs otherwise run on paper registers, Excel and generic data-collection tools (CommCare/ODK), which do not encode stepped-care rules, supervision workflows or validated outcome trajectories, so donors cannot see quality and ministries cannot justify budget lines. Funding shocks then hit the least-documented programs first.

**Intervention.** An offline-first, open-core 'practice and quality system' for task-shared mental health care. Per-client measurement-based care: PHQ-9/GAD-7 and locally validated tools such as SSQ-14, scored and trended per session. A stepped-care rule engine: automatic flags for non-response, deterioration or suicide risk, each generating a time-bound referral task. Structured supervision workflows: caseload summaries for group peer supervision, random session sampling against fidelity checklists, specialist escalation queues, and supervisor-to-counsellor ratio tracking. Program-level outcome reporting that ministries and funders can audit. Differentiation: EQUIP measures training-stage competencies and EMPOWER delivers training plus pilot-scale peer supervision, whereas this runs the ongoing service and makes quality legible to the people who fund it; versus CommCare, the clinical logic, supervision ratios and outcome definitions are first-class rather than rebuilt per project. It is useful with no AI at all; any later AI use would be limited to summarising free-text case notes for supervisors and would not involve voice or speech.

**Business model.** Open-source core (so cash-poor programs and ministries are never locked out) with paid hosting, data-protection compliance, integrations and support sold to NGOs, ministries of health and donors on a per-site or per-counsellor basis. Funders increasingly require outcome data, which is exactly what the product produces, so the buyer is the program budget rather than the client. Honest caveat: the paying market shrank in 2025 with the aid cuts, so revenue should be anchored in multi-year philanthropic and multilateral funders (Global Fund-style mechanisms, Grand Challenges Canada, Wellcome) and in middle-income government programs, not in short grants.

**Outcomes.** Supervision coverage (% of counsellors receiving scheduled supervision each month; sustained supervisor-to-counsellor ratio); % of sessions with a recorded outcome measure; PHQ-9 response/remission rates versus published trial benchmarks; dropout before session 3; median time from screening to first session; % of suicide-risk flags with a documented referral within 24-48 hours; fidelity checklist pass rate on sampled sessions; cost per client in remission; and, for the field, the share of programs that survive a funding shock with auditable outcome data.

### Competitive landscape (crowdedness 4/10)

- **WHO/UNICEF EQUIP (Ensuring Quality in Psychosocial and Mental Health Care).** Free digital platform plus a compendium of competency-assessment tools (ENACT, WeACT, GroupACT, Foundational Helping Skills) that trainers use to observe role-plays, rate non-specialist providers' competencies, and see cohort-level skill gaps. Framed by WHO/UNICEF as a 'competency-based training and supervision system' (Kohrt et al., Lancet Psychiatry 2025, 12(1):67-80; WHO compendium 2026, iris.who.int/handle/10665/385848). *Shortfall:* It is a trainee-and-trainer tool, not a service system: it has no client records, no per-client outcome measures (PHQ-9/GAD-7 trajectories), no suicide-risk or non-response rules, no referral tasks with deadlines, no caseload or supervisor-to-counsellor ratio tracking, and no programme-level outcome reporting. Data is per-trainee role-play, not per-client real-world care. Risk to note honestly: WHO has published competency-based supervision guidance and could extend EQUIP toward in-service supervision, so a new product should integrate EQUIP's competency items rather than compete with them.
- **EMPOWER (Harvard Global Mental Health lab / Sangath).** Digital training for brief psychological treatments (e.g. the Healthy Activity Program), competency certification using EQUIP-type tools, and peer-supervision support for community health workers, with Grand Challenges Canada funding. *Shortfall:* Training-centric and research-led: it is not a multi-tenant practice system that an NGO or ministry can adopt, does not run measurement-based care per client, does not encode stepped-care escalation, is India-focused, and is not shipped as an open-source product. Its peer-supervision component is the closest thing to the proposed intervention but has not left pilot scale.
- **CommCare (Dimagi), with ODK Collect and KoboToolbox as the generic alternatives.** Offline-first mobile form and case-management platform (open-source server, BSD-3) used by NGOs to build data-collection apps per project; ODK (XLSForm) and KoboToolbox do the same for surveys. Exposes a REST export API (verified: corehq/apps/api/resources/v0_5.py). *Shortfall:* It is a toolkit, not a practice system: PHQ-9 scoring, response/remission definitions, risk flags, referral SLAs, supervision sampling and supervisor ratios all have to be rebuilt per project (a GitHub code search found zero public PHQ-9 CommCare XForms). No mental-health content, no fidelity workflow, no cross-programme comparable outcome report. Hosted CommCare is priced per user, which matters after the 2025 aid cuts. The honest implication: any new product must either import from CommCare/Kobo/ODK or prove incremental value over a well-built CommCare app.
- **Medic Community Health Toolkit (CHT).** Open-source (AGPL-3.0) offline-first framework for community-health-worker apps with tasks, targets and supervisor roles (verified in cht-core default app_settings), used by ministries for CHW programmes. *Shortfall:* Designed around maternal, child and infectious-disease CHW visits; no psychometric instruments, score trending, stepped-care rules, fidelity checklists or supervision-ratio logic. Building a counselling programme on CHT means a bespoke configuration project with Medic or a partner, and the result is still a CHW app rather than a quality-and-supervision system for counsellors.
- **OpenMRS / MSF LIME EMR (mhGAP forms) and DHIS2.** Facility electronic medical records and national HMIS. MSF's LIME EMR (OpenMRS 3 distribution) ships 26 core forms including mental health (MHPSS/mhGAP v2) and syncs to DHIS2 Tracker via OpenFn; OpenMRS core has 1,957 stars, DHIS2 core 360 (all verified). *Shortfall:* Facility- and clinician-centred: heavy to deploy, needs a clinic and connectivity, and does not model lay counsellors, community sessions, supervision, fidelity sampling or benchmarked outcome trajectories. DHIS2 carries aggregates, so quality and escalation are invisible at the client level.
- **Friendship Bench in-house tooling and its spin-off Inuka.** Friendship Bench runs problem-solving therapy by trained lay 'grandmother' counsellors with SSQ-14/PHQ-9 screening and internal digital registers; Inuka (Netherlands) productised the method as chat-based coaching by trained lay coaches for employers and insurers. *Shortfall:* The programme's own 2026 implementation review names 'limited supervision' and 'inadequate incentives' as barriers and finds cost-effectiveness only under enhanced supervision; its tooling is programme-specific and not offered to other implementers. Inuka serves high-income employer markets with online chat, not offline supervision of LMIC counsellors. Friendship Bench was directly hit by the 2025 USAID cuts, which is the demand signal for auditable outcomes.
- **StrongMinds in-house monitoring (group IPT).** Lay-facilitator group interpersonal therapy for women in Uganda and Zambia with PHQ-9 at intake and discharge and an internal M&E system. *Shortfall:* Internal, single-programme, not a product; outcome definitions are the programme's own; no cross-programme comparability; supervision and fidelity records are not part of what funders can audit.
- **High-income measurement-based-care SaaS: Mirah, Blueprint, Greenspace, Owl.** Clinician-facing platforms that administer PHQ-9/GAD-7 and other PROMs, trend scores, alert on deterioration and report outcomes to US/Canadian payers and health systems. *Shortfall:* English, online-only, HIPAA/US-payer oriented, priced per clinician at levels no LMIC NGO can pay; no lay-counsellor supervision, fidelity sampling, supervisor ratios, humanitarian data-governance or offline sync; several have pivoted toward AI note-taking. GitHub shows no credible open-source equivalent (only 0-star student projects), which is a genuine gap.
- **Lyssn (AI fidelity rating of recorded sessions) and mindLAMP (BIDMC open-source research platform).** Lyssn rates psychotherapy fidelity (e.g. motivational interviewing) from session audio for US providers; mindLAMP is an open-source, actively maintained (pushed 2026-11-01) digital-phenotyping and survey platform for research clinics. *Shortfall:* Lyssn depends on audio recording, consent, bandwidth and English-language models, and is speech-based, which the sponsor has excluded as a direction. mindLAMP is a research instrument, not a programme practice or supervision system, and has no stepped-care or supervision logic.

**What remains unsolved.** Nobody sells or open-sources the service-stage quality loop for task-shared mental health care: (1) a per-client measurement-based-care record for lay counsellors with standard, auditable outcome definitions (PHQ-9 response, remission, dropout before session 3) comparable across programmes and against published trial benchmarks; (2) stepped-care rules that turn suicide risk, deterioration or non-response into time-bound referral tasks with an SLA and a specialist escalation queue; (3) supervision as a tracked process (coverage per counsellor per month, supervisor-to-counsellor ratio, random session sampling against EQUIP/ENACT-aligned fidelity checklists) rather than ad hoc WhatsApp groups; (4) continuity between EQUIP's training-stage competencies and in-service performance; (5) offline-first operation with data protection acceptable to ministries and humanitarian donors. EQUIP stops at training, EMPOWER is pilot-scale and training-centric, CommCare/Kobo/ODK/CHT leave every piece of clinical and supervision logic to be rebuilt per project, EMRs serve clinics not benches, and HIC MBC vendors are the wrong price, language and connectivity. Two things software cannot solve and should not claim to: it cannot create scarce supervisor hours (it can only triage them), and the evidence that digital supervision improves client outcomes is itself thin, so a launch partner evaluation is part of the product, not an afterthought.

**Differentiated wedge.** Lead with the supervisor, not the counsellor, and with a work queue, not a dashboard: a 'risk-and-supervision ledger' for task-shared mental-health programmes. Concretely, the first release does four things no incumbent does together: (a) every PHQ-9 item-9 or deterioration/non-response trigger opens a referral task with a 24-48 h SLA, an escalation path to a named specialist queue, and an audit trail of closure; (b) supervision is scheduled and measured (monthly coverage per counsellor, supervisor-to-counsellor ratio, random session sampling scored on EQUIP/ENACT-aligned fidelity checklists, so EQUIP competencies continue into in-service care instead of ending at certification); (c) outcome definitions are fixed and funder-grade (response, remission, dropout before session 3, time-to-first-session) and reported per site against published Friendship Bench/PM+/Thinking Healthy benchmarks; (d) it ingests session and score data from the CommCare, Kobo and ODK forms programmes already run (via their export APIs) as well as from its own offline-first PWA forms, so adoption is additive rather than rip-and-replace. Target the first customers precisely: programmes with 50-500 counsellors that lost USAID funding in 2025 and must re-pitch to philanthropic or multilateral funders with outcome evidence, and GCC/Wellcome grantees with reporting obligations. Open-core (AGPL) with paid hosting, data-protection packaging and integrations. Explicitly no voice, speech or recording; any later AI is limited to summarising free-text case notes for supervisors.

## Skilled migrants and refugees cannot navigate credential recognition and licensure, so they work far below their training

*Id:* `migrant-credential-recognition` · *Merged from:* Skilled migrants' and refugees' qualifications are not recognised, so they work far below 

**Who.** Tertiary-educated immigrants and refugees in high-income destination countries, above all those whose degree was earned abroad: foreign-educated nurses, physicians, engineers, teachers and accountants from the Philippines, India, Nigeria, Syria, Ukraine, Afghanistan, Venezuela and Sudan living in the US, Canada and the EU (worst in Greece, Italy, Spain). Also refugees who fled without transcripts or whose universities no longer exist (Syria, Afghanistan, Sudan), and refugee-hosting countries in Africa (Uganda, Kenya, Zambia) where recognition capacity is a handful of part-time evaluators. Women and Black and Latino foreign-educated graduates face higher underutilisation (Migration Policy Institute).

**Scale.** US: about 2 million college-educated immigrants were in jobs requiring at most a high-school diploma or unemployed in 2019 (MPI); MPI's 2016 'Untapped Talent' study (2009-13 Census data) put forgone wages at $39.4 billion/year and lost taxes above $10 billion/year; 29% of foreign-educated immigrant graduates were underutilised vs 21% of US-educated ones. EU: 39.4% of employed non-EU citizens with tertiary education were over-qualified in 2023, almost double the ~21% rate for nationals; Greece 69.6%, Italy 64.1%, Spain 56.0% (Eurostat, July 2024); 39.6% in 2024, down from 45.9% in 2014. Canada: 25.8% of immigrants aged 25-64 with a foreign degree worked in jobs needing at most high school in 2021, twice the Canadian-born/educated rate; only 36.5% of immigrants with a foreign nursing degree worked as RNs or closely related jobs (Statistics Canada, 2021 Census). Licensure for an internationally educated nurse in British Columbia can cost about $3,000 (C.D. Howe Institute, 2024). Refugee recognition capacity is tiny: Uganda's UNESCO Qualifications Passport programme (from May 2024) drew 220 applications, assessed 60 and issued 49 passports with eight part-time evaluators; passports are issued only in Zambia, Kenya and Uganda (The Observer, Uganda). Note: figures were taken from search-result excerpts of the named primary sources, as direct page fetches were blocked.

**Why solutions fail.** Credential evaluation services (WES, ECE, CGFNS, ENIC-NARIC centres) are document-centric, paid per report, and answer only 'what is this degree equivalent to'; they do not tell a nurse from Lagos the licensure sequence, cost, exam, bridging course and timeline in Ohio versus Ontario, and people often pay for several evaluations for different purposes. Licensing is fragmented across hundreds of state/provincial regulators and professional bodies with no shared machine-readable data, so settlement agencies (Upwardly Global, ACCES, IMPRINT) hand-maintain PDFs and web pages that go stale. Employers cannot interpret foreign credentials and default to 'local experience required'. Refugees without documents fall outside the system entirely; the UNESCO Qualifications Passport exists but has been issued in only a few countries, in the dozens, constrained by evaluator capacity. Information failure compounds real barriers (language, exam cost, bridging seats): MPI found English proficiency the strongest predictor of underemployment, so pathway guidance must integrate language-level planning rather than treat credentials in isolation.

**Intervention.** An open, pathway-centric credential navigator: a structured, versioned, machine-readable graph mapping (origin country, institution, qualification) to destination-country comparability, and (target occupation, jurisdiction) to the exact licensure sequence — prerequisites, documents, exams, bridging programmes, fees, statutory timelines, appeal routes — with a cost/time estimator, a personal checklist that generates document requests to the issuing institution, and an employer-facing 'credential explainer' page a candidate can attach to applications. Novel because (1) it models the occupation-by-jurisdiction pathway, not the paper, so users see total cost and months-to-licence before paying anyone; (2) pathway data is open and crowd-verified by settlement navigators and licensing bodies with change-tracking, instead of locked in evaluators' proprietary files; (3) it is built for the no-documents case, structuring UNESCO Qualifications Passport-style interview evidence into a portable record. It needs no AI to be useful; LLM extraction can merely speed ingestion of regulator pages, with human review.

**Business model.** Free for individuals (never sell applicant data). Revenue from employers and staffing firms (hospital systems recruiting internationally educated nurses pay for verified pathway status and credential explainers), settlement agencies and immigrant-serving nonprofits (SaaS caseload tools), and licensing bodies and governments (pre-screening that reduces incomplete applications; Canada's Foreign Credential Recognition Program and EU integration funds already pay for this kind of work). Open pathway data is a public good; the paid tier is workflow, verification and employer integrations.

**Outcomes.** Share of registered users employed in an occupation matching their qualification level within 12 months (versus ~25-40% over-qualification baselines); months from arrival to licence application and to licence for tracked cohorts; wage change six and twelve months after a pathway is completed; evaluation fees avoided and pathway abandonments prevented (dollars saved per user); regulator-side reduction in incomplete or rejected applications; number of verified pathway records maintained and their staleness (median days since last regulator check).

### Competitive landscape (crowdedness 6/10)

- **World Education Services (WES) — credential evaluation, Gateway Program, Global Talent Bridge.** Nonprofit evaluator for the US and Canada. Sells document-by-document and course-by-course equivalency reports (roughly US$100–250 per report plus delivery), a free Degree Equivalency quick-lookup, the WES Gateway Program (interview/alternative-evidence assessment for displaced people from a short list of eligible countries who cannot obtain documents), and Global Talent Bridge (free webinars, 'pathways' guides, and grants via the WES Mariam Assefa Fund). *Shortfall:* Answers 'what is this degree comparable to', not 'what is the sequence, cost and time to licence in Ohio vs Ontario'. Many regulators do not accept WES reports (nursing requires CGFNS/NNAS; medicine requires ECFMG/MCC), so applicants pay for multiple evaluations. Equivalency logic is proprietary and unversioned. Gateway is limited to listed countries and modest capacity. No cross-jurisdiction comparison, no language-level planning, no employer-facing explanation.
- **CGFNS International (US) and the National Nursing Assessment Service (NNAS, Canada) — profession-specific gatekeepers.** CGFNS issues the VisaScreen certificate (required under US immigration law for most foreign nurse visas) and Credentials Evaluation Service reports used by most state boards; NNAS (https://www.nnas.ca/) is the mandatory single entry point for internationally educated nurses in every Canadian province except Quebec, producing an advisory report sent to the provincial regulator. *Shortfall:* Single profession, document-centric, slow and expensive; they are a step in the pathway, not a navigator of it. They give no comparison across states/provinces, no total-cost or months-to-licence estimate, no bridging-programme or language-test planning, and no help for people without documents. Their monopoly position means they have no incentive to open data.
- **Upwardly Global.** US nonprofit (since 2000) offering free job-search coaching, an online career-skills platform, employer partnerships, and state-by-state licensing guides for professions such as nursing, medicine, pharmacy, engineering, accounting and teaching. *Shortfall:* Coaching-intensive and capacity bound; US only. Licensing guides are hand-maintained web pages/PDFs with no versioning, change tracking, or machine-readable structure, so they go stale as states change rules (and 2023–2025 saw an unusually fast wave of IMG and IEN rule changes). No cost/time modelling, no cross-state comparison, no refugee no-documents flow.
- **IMPRINT coalition and the Welcome Back Initiative (WBI).** IMPRINT is a US coalition of immigrant-professional-integration organisations (Upwardly Global, WES Global Talent Bridge, WBI and others) doing advocacy, research and practitioner guides. WBI (https://www.welcomebackinitiative.org/) runs a network of roughly ten state-based centres that counsel internationally trained health workers through US licensure, since 2001. *Shortfall:* In-person, local, grant-dependent; knowledge lives in counsellors' heads and in static guides. No shared data infrastructure across the coalition, which is exactly the gap the proposed intervention targets.
- **Anerkennung in Deutschland (BIBB/BMBF) with the Recognition Finder, Anabin (KMK), BQ-Portal and the ZSBA.** Germany's official recognition portal: the Recognition Finder maps an occupation and place of residence to the competent authority, the procedure, required documents and legal basis, in about 11 languages; Anabin (https://anabin.kmk.org/) is a public database of foreign institutions and degrees with comparability ratings; BQ-Portal profiles foreign vocational qualifications; the ZSBA counsels applicants still abroad. Backed by the 2012 Recognition Act and the 2023 Skilled Immigration Act. *Shortfall:* Germany only and German-law only; no cross-country comparison for someone choosing between Germany, Austria and the Netherlands. Gives procedure and documents but no total cost, no realistic time distribution, no bridging/adaptation-course capacity, no language-level planning beyond stating the threshold. Anabin has no API and its coverage of institutions from conflict countries is thin. No refugee no-documents workflow beyond pointing to the EQPR/'Qualifikationsanalyse'. Not open data: content cannot be reused by third parties.
- **UNESCO Qualifications Passport (UQP) and Council of Europe European Qualifications Passport for Refugees (EQPR).** Interview-based, evidence-light assessment of a refugee's qualifications producing a standardised document (valid five years) that describes highest qualification, work experience and language proficiency; EQPR is run by Council of Europe with ENIC-NARIC centres and NOKUT, UQP by UNESCO in refugee-hosting countries (Zambia pilot 2019, Iraq, Colombia region, Uganda from 2024, Kenya). *Shortfall:* Capacity-limited by trained evaluators and donor funding; issued only in a handful of countries; not a licence to practise (a passport does not satisfy a nursing regulator); no digital, portable, machine-readable record that downstream regulators or employers can consume; no connection to a licensure pathway afterwards.
- **US public licensing datasets: CareerOneStop License Finder (US DOL), NCSL National Occupational Licensing Database, WVU Knee Center CSOR, Institute for Justice 'License to Work'.** CareerOneStop aggregates state licence records (name, agency, description, requirements text) searchable by occupation and state, with a free Web API. NCSL's database (https://www.ncsl.org/labor-and-employment/the-national-occupational-licensing-database) tabulates requirements (education, exam, fees, experience) for roughly 48 occupations across states. CSOR (https://csorwvu.com/) and IJ (https://ij.org/report/license-to-work-3/) publish research datasets on licensing burdens for hundreds / 102 occupations respectively. *Shortfall:* None has a foreign-credential dimension: they describe requirements for a domestically trained applicant, say nothing about CGFNS/ECFMG/NNAS steps, accredited-equivalency rules, English tests, or alternative IMG pathways. Requirement text is unstructured, often stale (state agencies update sporadically), no fees/time for internationally educated applicants, no change history. Good as a baseline feed, useless as a navigator.
- **Canada: CICIC (Canadian Information Centre for International Credentials) plus provincial Fairness Commissioners (Ontario OFC, BC, Manitoba, Nova Scotia, Quebec) and the federal Foreign Credential Recognition Program.** CICIC (run by CMEC) maintains directories of assessment services and regulated occupations by province with links to each regulator; Fairness Commissioners audit regulators and publish annual 'Fair Registration Practices' data (numbers of international applicants, time to decision, outcomes) under fair-access legislation; ESDC's FCR Program funds projects and loans (e.g., Windmill Microlending, https://windmillmicrolending.org/, which has lent to well over 10,000 newcomers for exam/licensing costs — from memory). *Shortfall:* Directories, not pathways: the user is handed a list of links and must assemble the sequence, cost and timeline alone. No cross-province comparison even though the choice of province is the single most consequential decision for an IEN or IMG; no machine-readable or versioned content; fairness data is published as PDFs per regulator and never joined to pathway information.
- **International nurse recruiters and sponsors (Avant Healthcare Professionals, Conexus MedStaff, Health Carousel International, O'Grady Peyton).** For-profit agencies that select nurses abroad (Philippines, India, Nigeria, Kenya, Caribbean), pay for and manage CGFNS, NCLEX, English tests, VisaScreen and EB-3 immigration, then place them with US hospitals under 2–3 year contracts. *Shortfall:* Serve the employer, not the nurse: opaque contracts, breach fees often above US$20,000, wage differentials, and selection of the easiest candidates. Nothing for nurses already in-country on family/refugee status, nothing for other professions, nothing for Canada/EU, and no public data. Their fees are evidence that money exists for pathway navigation; their lock-in is evidence that a neutral, free navigator is needed.
- **EU-level tools: Regulated Professions Database (RegProf), ENIC-NARIC network, EU Skills Profile Tool for Third Country Nationals, ESCO/Europass.** RegProf lists every regulated profession per EU/EEA country with competent authorities and recognition-decision statistics under Directive 2005/36/EC; ENIC-NARIC centres (https://www.enic-naric.net/) publish country education-system profiles and do academic recognition; the Skills Profile Tool (https://ec.europa.eu/migrantskills/) is a free multilingual self-assessment used by NGOs and public employment services since 2017; ESCO/Europass provide occupation/skill taxonomies and digital credentials. An EU Talent Pool platform for third-country nationals was adopted in 2024–2025 (status not verified here). *Shortfall:* RegProf is a legal registry, not a guide: no steps, costs, timelines, adaptation measures or language thresholds, and it is frequently out of date for individual countries. ENIC-NARIC answers academic comparability, not professional licensure, and quality varies wildly between centres (Greece, Italy, Spain — the worst over-qualification countries — have among the weakest services). The Skills Profile Tool produces a self-declared profile that no regulator consumes. No cross-country comparison, no versioning, no refugee no-documents record.
- **Skills-profiling and refugee-mobility startups/NGOs: SkillLab, Talent Beyond Boundaries, TalentLift, Kiron; and Credential Engine (open credential registry).** SkillLab (Amsterdam) is a mobile skills-profiling tool mapping work histories to ESCO, licensed to NGOs and employment services; Talent Beyond Boundaries (https://www.talentbeyondboundaries.org/) and TalentLift Canada move refugees via skilled-worker visas with employer sponsors; Kiron offers online study for refugees; Credential Engine (https://credentialengine.org/) runs an open, CTDL-based registry of US credentials (degrees, certificates, some licences) with a public API. *Shortfall:* SkillLab profiles skills, not licensure pathways; TBB is a labour-mobility matchmaker for a few hundred people a year with heavy employer-side work; Credential Engine describes US credentials but not foreign-to-domestic comparability or licensure sequences for internationally educated applicants. None of them maintains a versioned occupation × jurisdiction pathway graph. (Several LLM 'immigration assistant' chatbots launched 2023–2025; none observed with structured, cited, change-tracked pathway data — hedged, not verified.)

## Children in LMICs cannot read by age 10 because instruction and reading materials are not in a language or at a level they can decode

*Id:* `decodable-readers-language-of-instruction` · *Merged from:* Children in low- and middle-income countries cannot read by age 10, in large part because 

**Who.** Primary-school children in low- and middle-income countries, overwhelmingly in Sub-Saharan Africa (highest learning poverty) and South Asia; most acutely children whose home language differs from the official language of instruction — e.g. Francophone West Africa (French from grade 1), multilingual Kenya, Uganda, Tanzania, Nigeria, Ethiopia, Mozambique, DR Congo, the Sahel, India's tribal-language belts, Pakistan, Papua New Guinea. Rural, poor and minority-language children are hit hardest. The second affected group is their teachers, who often have no graded, decodable text in the local language to teach from.

**Scale.** About 70% of 10-year-olds in LMICs could not read and understand a simple text in 2022, up from 57% pre-pandemic; 89% in Sub-Saharan Africa; estimated lifetime earnings loss of $21 trillion, about 17% of today's global GDP (World Bank/UNESCO/UNICEF/FCDO/USAID/Gates, 'State of Global Learning Poverty: 2022 Update', June 2022; part of the 70% is modelled, not measured). UNESCO GEM Report: 40% of the world's population lacks access to education in a language they speak or understand ('If you don't understand, how can you learn?', 2016, still cited by UNESCO in 2026); in Côte d'Ivoire 55% of grade-5 pupils who spoke the test language at home learned basic reading vs 25% who did not. UNESCO 2026 International Mother Language Day: only a few hundred of ~7,000 languages are used in education and fewer than 100 are represented digitally. Supply is thin: the Global Book Alliance (2018) targeted 50,000 titles in 100 languages; by 2021 the Global Digital Library held just over 6,000 titles in ~90 languages, mostly translated storybooks rather than decodable texts aligned to a national phonics sequence. 272 million children and youth were out of school in 2023 (UNESCO 2025 SDG 4 Scorecard). The number of children with zero home-language books is not reliably quantified ('millions', Global Book Alliance).

**Why solutions fail.** The evidence is unusually clear — the 2023 GEEAP 'Smart Buys' report rates structured pedagogy (scripted lessons + materials + teacher support) and teaching at the right level as 'Great Buys' and hardware-only technology a 'Bad Buy' — but scale-up fails on the materials leg. Ministries adopt mother-tongue policies for early grades (Kenya, Uganda, Ethiopia, Senegal, Nigeria, Mozambique) without a supply of grade-appropriate decodable and leveled texts per language: commercial publishers see no market in languages with a few hundred thousand speakers; donor projects (Tusome, SHRP, RARA) produce titles for one language set and end; open repositories (Global Digital Library, StoryWeaver, African Storybook, Bloom) are translation-driven, so titles are not sequenced to the grapheme-phoneme order a given orthography and curriculum teach. Books that reach schools are often locked in cupboards or pitched above children's decoding level; 'one textbook per 3-10 children' persists. RTI's Uganda local-language RCT showed P4 learners 1.5-6 times more likely to read 40+ words per minute in programme schools, but that depended on bespoke materials never generalised to other languages.

**Intervention.** An open 'decodable reader factory' web platform. For each language, encode its orthography (grapheme inventory, syllable structure, tone marking) and the national or project phonics scope-and-sequence; the system then validates and levels any submitted text against the exact set of graphemes, syllable types and high-frequency words taught up to each week, flags words not yet decodable, and assembles print-ready low-ink A5/A4 booklets (plus phone-readable HTML) from community-authored or CC-BY stories (Global Digital Library, StoryWeaver, African Storybook corpora). Native-speaker reviewers and teachers approve texts through a review workflow; ministries get a per-language gap map of which weeks of the sequence have no texts. Novelty: existing repositories level by vocabulary and sentence length, not decodability against a specific orthography and curriculum order, and none give a ministry a coverage gap map. The core value — orthography-aware leveling, review workflow, print-ready output — needs no AI; LLM drafting may be offered for higher-resource languages only, behind mandatory human review, since model quality in most target languages is poor. No speech, voice, wearable or hardware component.

**Business model.** Core platform and generated texts remain openly licensed (CC-BY) so governments and NGOs can print freely. Revenue from: ministries and donor-funded reading programmes (World Bank, GPE, USAID, FCDO already budget for materials development) paying for language onboarding, custom level packs and gap analyses; publishers paying for the leveling/validation API; print partners paying for print-ready layout bundles; philanthropic grants for the lowest-resource languages. Children's and teachers' access to the texts themselves is never paywalled.

**Outcomes.** Oral reading fluency and reading-comprehension gains on EGRA in partner vs comparison schools; share of grade-2/3 children reaching the national fluency benchmark; titles available per language at each level of the scope-and-sequence, and weeks of the sequence with zero texts; books per child in home language actually in classrooms; cost per 1,000 printed pages delivered; teacher usage (texts read per week per class) from spot-check logs.

### Competitive landscape (crowdedness 4/10)

- **Bloom (SIL Global): Decodable Reader Tool, Leveled Reader Tool, Bloom Library, Bloom Reader.** Open-source (MIT) book-making desktop app from SIL. Verified from source and docs: a literacy specialist defines per-collection 'decodable stages' (letters AND letter combinations — digraphs, vowel length, nasality, clusters, tone diacritics — plus sight words and optional allowed-word lists), and 'leveled reader' levels (max words per sentence/page/book, max unique words per book, max glyphs per word, averages). The tool highlights words in a book that are not decodable at the chosen stage, suggests decodable words from sample texts, and lets specialists ship 'Reader Template Bloom Packs' to authors. Books publish to bloomlibrary.org (Parse backend) and to the Bloom Reader Android app / BloomPUB. Decodability is letter/letter-combination + sight-word membership; it does not model syllable-type progression (CV, CVC, CCV) or curriculum weeks, and tone is handled only by listing tone-marked combinations as 'letters'. Honest limits: in the first 10-15 weeks of any sequence almost no existing story is decodable, so the gap map will show mostly empty cells and the platform must support constrained authoring, not only filtering; per-language tokenisation and syllable rules still need a linguist; and reading outcomes depend on print/distribution logistics the software cannot control. ## Teachers are posted to the wrong schools, leaving rural and poor schools understaffed despite national supply

*Id:* `teacher-misallocation` · *Merged from:* Teachers are posted to the wrong schools: misallocation leaves rural and poor schools with

**Who.** Children in rural, remote and poor schools in South Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa who sit in single-teacher or severely under-staffed schools while urban schools hold surplus staff. Concretely: roughly 4 million pupils in India's ~111,000 single-teacher schools (concentrated in Madhya Pradesh, Andhra Pradesh, Uttar Pradesh, Jharkhand, Himachal); pupils in remote schools in Tanzania, Malawi, The Gambia, Sierra Leone, Ghana, Uganda and Mozambique; and the teachers themselves, who face opaque, often patronage-driven transfer systems and hardship postings without support. Secondary-level subject gaps (maths, science) are a further layer.

**Scale.** Globally, 44 million additional primary and secondary teachers are needed by 2030 (12.9M primary, 31.1M secondary), with 15M in Sub-Saharan Africa and 7.8M in South Asia; primary-teacher attrition nearly doubled from 4.62% in 2015 to 9.06% in 2022 (UNESCO / Teacher Task Force, Global Report on Teachers, 2024). A large share of within-country gaps is distribution, not supply: a World Bank brief on Tanzania, Malawi and The Gambia estimates 28% of between-school staffing variation cannot be explained by enrolment, with remote schools frequently understaffed; in Tanzania only 36% of schools fell in the acceptable pupil-teacher-ratio range. India's UDISE+ 2023-24 reports 110,971 single-teacher schools serving nearly 40 lakh (~4 million) students, while 31,981 teachers were posted to 12,964 schools with zero enrolment; in 2024-25 single-teacher schools still numbered 104,125 and zero-enrolment schools 7,993 (Ministry of Education UDISE+, via PIB and Careers360). A 2026 UNICEF Innocenti note on Sierra Leone attributes rural and gender disparities partly to uneven deployment. Caveat: the 28% and 36% figures were seen only in excerpts (direct fetches blocked), and the 44M global figure mixes supply and attrition needs with distribution.

**Why solutions fail.** Policy and donor attention goes to headline supply (recruit more teachers, national PTR) rather than equitable distribution within districts. Transfer and posting decisions are discretionary, politically mediated and sometimes corrupt, so teachers migrate toward towns and schools with influence; hardship allowances are small or unpaid. School-census data (UDISE+, annual school censuses) exist but are not connected to the moment a posting or transfer is decided, and 'rationalisation' drives are manual, periodic, contested by unions and reversed. A few Indian states run online counselling-based transfer rounds, but these optimise individual preference lists, not school-level deficits, and provide no public audit of fairness. The 2024 UNESCO report notes attrition data are missing in many countries, so planners lack the forward view needed to pre-empt vacancies.

**Intervention.** An open teacher-allocation and transfer-fairness engine. It ingests public school-census data (school-level enrolment by grade, sanctioned and in-position teachers by subject, coordinates, school type), computes each school's norm-based entitlement (e.g., India's RTE 30:1 primary / 35:1 upper primary, subject norms for secondary, or a country's own PTR bands), produces an auditable district-level surplus/deficit map, then runs a constrained optimisation (OR-Tools) proposing transfer plans that minimise relocation distance and disruption subject to subject match, gender balance, hardship rotation, spousal and medical constraints. A simulation mode lets officials and unions see the effect of a rule change before a transfer round, and a public fairness report follows it. ## Smallholder grain farmers lose harvest in storage and 'sell low, buy high' because they cannot judge when storing pays

*Id:* `smallholder-store-or-sell` · *Merged from:* Smallholder grain farmers in sub-Saharan Africa lose a fifth of their harvest after harves

**Who.** Smallholder cereal (maize, sorghum, millet, rice) farming households across sub-Saharan Africa, concentrated in West Africa (Nigeria, Ghana, Senegal, Mali, Burkina Faso), East Africa (Kenya, Tanzania, Uganda, Ethiopia) and Southern Africa (Malawi, Zambia, Mozambique, Zimbabwe); secondarily smallholders in South Asia. The same households are net food buyers in the lean season, so the loss hits them twice: as lost product and as higher repurchase prices. Women, who do most on-farm storage and small-lot selling, and farmer cooperatives/aggregators that hold pooled grain are the operational decision-makers. Agri-lenders and MFIs that finance harvest-time storage loans are the institutions whose decisions set whether a farmer can afford to wait.

**Scale.** Global: FAO SDG indicator 12.3.1a (Food Loss Index) puts food lost between harvest and retail at 13.3% in 2023 (13.0% in 2015), i.e. no progress toward SDG 12.3; cereals and pulses 8.4%. APHLIS estimates 10-23% of African cereal production is lost post-harvest; the World Bank (2011) valued SSA grain losses at about US$4 billion/year, the annual calories of 48 million people (both cited in World Bank working paper 108109, 2016). Household-survey maize losses are lower (1.4-5.9%, Kaminski & Christiaensen 2014, LSMS-ISA), so 5-20% is the honest band. The price-timing loss is better measured: Cardell & Michelson (AJAE, 2023), using WFP retail prices from 1,038 markets in 30 African countries over 2000-2021, find average harvest-to-lean maize price returns of 41.7% in single-season countries (35-39% double-season), yet prices fail to rise in 16.3% of market-years (up to 50% in Mauritania) and fall ~11.5% when they fall, so credit-constrained farmers rationally sell at harvest. Burke, Bergquist & Miguel (NBER w24476, 2018) show Kenyan farmers systematically sell low and buy high; harvest-time credit yielded a 28% return. Hermetic storage works (Ngoma et al. 2023 meta-analysis: conventional bags show 23x more weight loss, 42x more insects) but adoption remains low (Okori et al. 2022, 306 Ugandan farmers). Primary FAO/APHLIS/World Bank sites were unreachable; figures verified via hosted copies.

**Why solutions fail.** (1) Hardware-first solutions (PICS hermetic bags, metal silos, solar cold rooms) need upfront cash and physical distribution, so adoption stays low, and they do nothing about the sell-timing decision. (2) Market-price SMS/app services (Esoko, M-Farm, national MIS) broadcast spot prices but not the decision that matters: the expected net return to storing this lot for N weeks in this market, net of expected storage loss, mold/aflatoxin risk and the farmer's cost of cash. Cardell & Michelson show harvest prices only partly predict whether storage will pay, so raw price feeds are insufficient. (3) Warehouse-receipt systems and commodity exchanges reach only aggregated, graded volumes near cities. (4) APHLIS, the best loss dataset, is built for researchers and donors, not operations; its API returned 401 to automated access in a project reviewed. (5) Credit constraints (Burke et al.) mean information alone does not change behaviour unless it is wired into the lender's or cooperative's harvest-time financing decision.

**Intervention.** A store-or-sell decision and storage-risk engine for farmer cooperatives, aggregators, agri-lenders and extension services (B2B web app, with SMS/USSD digests to members rather than a consumer app). For each market x crop it builds the empirical distribution of harvest-to-lean-season returns from 20+ years of WFP market prices (Cardell & Michelson's method), combines it with APHLIS-derived expected storage loss by crop, storage type and climate zone, a humidity/temperature mold-risk score from open weather data, and the cooperative's or lender's cost of money, and outputs per-lot recommendations: sell now, store N weeks, store only in hermetic bags, or take a harvest-time loan against stored grain, always shown as probabilities with the historical failure base rate. It records outcomes (actual sale price, measured weight loss) to recalibrate. The novelty is operationalising a well-established academic result (storage returns are large on average but fail in a predictable minority of market-years) into a lot-level tool that prices loss risk, price risk and credit together; nothing in the market does this, and it needs only standard seasonal statistics, no AI.

**Business model.** Pay-side is the institution that gains from better timing: (a) agri-lenders and MFIs offering harvest-time storage loans (the tool improves repayment and lets them price loans by market-year risk); (b) cooperatives and aggregators (seasonal subscription per tonne stored); (c) hermetic-bag and silo distributors who want demand triggered by risk alerts; (d) NGOs and government extension on donor contracts funding a free farmer tier. Farmer-level data is never sold and recommendations are not tied to any buyer, preserving the social value. Risk: cooperatives have low willingness to pay, so lenders and distributors must anchor revenue.

**Outcomes.** Share of tracked lots sold in the lean season rather than at harvest; realised sale price vs harvest-week price net of measured storage loss (USD/tonne gained); measured grain weight loss (%) in tracked stores vs cooperative baseline; lender repayment rate on storage loans; calibration error (how often 'store' was recommended in a market-year when prices fell vs the historical 16.3% base rate); household lean-season grain purchases (kg) among member farmers.

(5) SMS/email reminders in the client's language and event-triggered re-screening (birth, job loss, pension age). (6) Outcome capture (award letters, dollars preserved) and organisation-level procedural-denial patterns benchmarked against CMS unwinding data. Novelty: retention and post-termination reversal as first-class workflows, statute-as-code fused with document generation, and an office-feedback loop that keeps rules honest. Useful without AI.

**Business model.** Free to claimants, never a share of the award. Payers are institutions that bear the cost of lost benefits: Medicaid managed-care plans (lose capitation when members are dropped and already fund SDOH/retention outreach), FQHCs and hospitals (uncompensated care, discharge delays for undocumented patients), Feeding America food banks (SNAP-outreach contracts draw a 50% federal match), legal aid, Continuums of Care and shelters (HUD CoC/ESG funds), reentry programmes, UK councils, housing associations and utilities (arrears reduction and social-tariff obligations; Policy in Practice reports £416 return per £1 for one council), credit unions (income maximisation reduces default) and employers (benefits-cliff guidance). Per-seat or per-member SaaS with outcome-based contracts tied to confirmed awards and reduced churn; free tier for small pantries; philanthropic funding for the open rules and form libraries. Honest risks: dependence on US policy cycles and squeezed navigator budgets, slow public procurement, strong alignment on Medicaid but weaker paying intermediary for SNAP; modest, durable rather than venture-scale.

**Outcomes.** Recertification/renewal completion rate and procedural-termination rate for tracked cases vs the partner's prior-year or CMS state baseline (e.g. 71% procedural share); share of procedural terminations reversed within the 90-day window; median days of benefit or coverage gap after closure; months of benefits preserved per client converted to dollars; confirmed awards per 100 screened households and annualised value verified by award letters; funnel eligible to submitted to decided to paid with median days per step; share of ABAWD-subject clients correctly moved to an exemption; WIC retention past ages 1 and 2; fair-hearing requests filed and won; for ID cases, days from intake to ID in hand and in-person trips per case; navigator hours per retained client; cost per $/£1 secured for the payer; downstream arrears, missed medications and food insecurity at 90 days. Not counted: sign-ups, screenings or calculations run.

## Smallholders get no field-level, crop-stage-specific early warning for pests, sowing windows, dry spells or harvest rain

*Id:* `field-level-agronomic-early-warning` · *Merged from:* Pest and disease outbreaks reach smallholders only after the damage is visible: no area-le; Weather-timing failures (false-start sowing, dry spells, harvest-time rain) and disaster l

**Who.** Rain-fed smallholders growing staples in food-insecure regions: maize growers in sub-Saharan Africa (roughly 300 million Africans depend on maize), rice/wheat growers in the Indo-Gangetic Plain and South/South-East Asia, and smallholders in Central America, who must decide when to sow, top-dress, spray and harvest without plot-level forecasts or pest warnings. Savary et al. (2019) found pest and pathogen losses are systematically higher in food-insecure hotspots (SSA, Indo-Gangetic Plain, WANA) than in surplus regions. In India about 125 million small and marginal farming households, half indebted, face monsoon variability with the thinnest buffers (NSS 77th round 2019: monthly agricultural-household income Rs 10,218 vs average outstanding loan Rs 74,121). Women and the poorest are hit hardest: they cannot afford reactive spraying or re-sowing and are last visited by extension agents (Kenya about 1 officer per 1,093 farmers vs FAO-recommended 1:400; India about 1 per 1,000 households). Feature-phone users are the excluded majority: GSMA 2024 reports smartphones at 54% of sub-Saharan connections and 53% of covered people not using mobile internet.

**Scale.** Pests: Savary et al., Nature Ecology & Evolution 2019 (989 assessments, 219 experts, 67 countries): global yield losses of 21.5% wheat, 30.0% rice, 22.6% maize, 17.2% potato, 21.4% soybean, highest in food-insecure hotspots. FAO (2021): up to 40% of global crop production lost to pests yearly; plant diseases cost over USD 220 billion/yr, invasive insects at least USD 70 billion/yr. FAO EWEA Jan-Mar 2018, citing CABI 2017: unmanaged fall armyworm could cause 21-53% annual maize loss in 12 African countries, 8.3-20.6 million t/yr worth USD 2.48-6.18 billion; Ugandan farmers in the Makerere/Zindi dataset self-report 31% average maize loss. Weather: FAO 2023 Impact of Disasters report: USD 3.8 trillion crop and livestock losses 1991-2021 (USD 123 billion/yr, 5% of agricultural GDP; Africa 7.7% share, Asia USD 1,720 billion absolute). WMO State of the Global Climate 2024: only half of countries have adequate multi-hazard early warning; 2024 brought Sahel floods and severe southern-African drought. Downstream, NCRB ADSI 2022: 11,290 farming-sector suicides in India, up 3.75% from 2021; NSS 2019: 50.2% of agricultural households indebted. Caveats: FAO 40% is a ceiling, Savary figures are expert-elicited, FAW tonnage is a 2017 potential-loss model, disaster losses are modelled, and suicide causation is multi-factor.

**Why solutions fail.** Both the pest and weather layers are generic, reactive or coarse. Plantix (>10 million downloads) and PlantVillage Nuru (about 65% accuracy on cassava, ~50,000 Kenyan farmers) diagnose a photo after symptoms are visible, when the outbreak has already spread. CABI Plantwise (about 90 countries, >5,000 clinics, 13,200 plant doctors) is a human diagnostic layer, not a forecast. FAO FAMEWS collects scouting data into a global map, but the alert rarely returns to the neighbouring village; DLIS/FAMEWS/EMPRES-i+ are loosely coupled stacks whose warnings are regionally right but spatially coarse, causing missed areas and blanket spraying that erode trust. On weather, national agromet advisories (India's Gramin Krishi Mausam Sewa SMS, FEWS NET outlooks) are district-level and generic, not tied to a plot and crop stage; commercial vendors (Ignitia, Tomorrow.io, defunct aWhere) sell forecasts, but a forecast is not a sowing decision, and raw model output 'requires expertise in binary formats, grid systems and map projections' (Open-Meteo). Tropical forecast skill is about two weeks while onset rules (Sivakumar 1988: 20 mm in 3 days, no 7-day dry spell in the next 30) need longer, so services overpromise or go silent. Smartphone apps exclude the offline half. Extension ratios of 1:1,000+ mean the human channel cannot scale, and safety nets fail on execution: PMFBY mandates settlement within 2 weeks with 12% penal interest after 30 days, yet delays persist and farmers borrow without cover.

**Intervention.** An open, rule-based, plot-level agronomic early-warning engine that turns free weather data plus neighbourhood scouting into dated, crop-stage-specific decisions, delivered as plain text to feature phones (SMS/USSD/WhatsApp) and a web console for extension agents and cooperatives. Each farmer registers plot location, crop and sowing date. Per plot and grid cell the engine computes, from Open-Meteo hourly/16-day ensemble forecasts and 80-year archive plus CHIRPS rainfall: (a) sowing windows via published onset criteria with an explicit '16-day view of a 30-day rule' honesty flag; (b) top-dressing, spray and harvest/drying windows from rain, wind and humidity; (c) pest pressure from deterministic published biophysical models (degree-day generation timing for fall armyworm and stem borers, infection-period rules for potato late blight and wheat rusts, breeding-suitability for locusts); (d) a dated weather-evidence record for insurance claims. Geotagged scouting reports (trap counts, larvae per plant, photo-verified sightings) upgrade a cell from 'conditions favourable' to 'confirmed, scout now' and push alerts to every registered farm within a configurable radius, with the economic threshold and IPM-first action. Every advisory states its rule and data; false-alarm and lead-time statistics are published; records export FAMEWS-compatible. Novelty: predictive and neighbourhood-level rather than reactive and individual, ships decisions not forecasts, treats one farmer's report as a public good for the next village, and needs no AI (1970s-2000s crop science and 1980s agro-meteorology). Fabregas, Kremer & Schilbach (Science 2019): phone-delivered agricultural information raises yields about 4% and input adoption 22% at near-zero marginal cost.

**Business model.** B2B2C; farmers never pay. Revenue from (a) cooperatives, FPOs and contract-farming firms paying per member-season for alerts and scouting dashboards; (b) governments, national met services and NGOs licensing district deployments and white-labelling the last mile of their advisory mandate, replacing paper surveillance; (c) index insurers, microfinance lenders and pest-index covers buying plot-level weather records and pest-pressure indices, since timely decisions reduce defaults and the evidence record cuts claim-verification cost; (d) input distributors paying for demand forecasts. Donors fund scouting networks in the first countries. The main variable cost is SMS, so telco partnerships for zero-rated USSD matter. Guardrails: no pay-per-spray or sponsored product recommendations, no sale of farmer location data, IPM-first thresholds, open model code and rules, and published alert accuracy.

**Outcomes.** Lead time in days between first confirmed detection and alert receipt by neighbouring farms, and before dry spells or harvest rain; share of alerted farmers who scouted within 72 hours and true-positive vs false-alarm rate; re-sowing (false-start) rate and seed/labour cost per hectare among users vs comparable non-users; share of sowing events inside a verified viable window; measured yield loss (crop-cut or recall) in alerted vs non-alerted villages; prophylactic sprays avoided per hectare; share of users on feature phones; number and settlement rate of insurance claims filed with the platform's weather record and time to settlement vs the 2-week/30-day PMFBY benchmarks; reports exported into national/FAO surveillance; season-over-season retention; and cost per farmer-season.

## Counterfeit and diluted seed, fertiliser and agrochemicals make modern inputs unprofitable

*Id:* `counterfeit-farm-inputs` · *Merged from:* Counterfeit and diluted farm inputs (seed, fertiliser, agrochemicals) make modern inputs u

**Who.** Smallholder farmers buying from local agro-input retailers in East and West Africa and South Asia, where inputs are repackaged from large bags into small unbranded lots. Over 70% of Africans living under USD 1.25/day are engaged in smallholder farming (IFAD 2011, cited in Ashour et al. 2019). The best-measured case is Uganda's maize belt (Bold et al. 2017; Ashour et al. 2019), but regulators in Kenya (PCPB, KEPHIS, KEBS), Tanzania, Nigeria, Ghana, India (CIB&RC, state seed certification) and Bangladesh all run anti-counterfeit programmes, indicating the same market structure. Victims are disproportionately the farmers least able to test inputs or absorb a failed season, plus farm workers and families exposed to mislabeled chemicals.

**Scale.** Bold, Kaizzi, Svensson and Yanagizawa-Drott, Quarterly Journal of Economics 2017 (369 urea samples from 129 randomly sampled retail shops in Uganda's two main maize regions, plus 30 hybrid-seed samples): on average 30% of nutrient is missing from fertiliser, 95% of samples are diluted by 10-50%, and hybrid maize seed contains less than 50% authentic seed. The mean rate of return on fertiliser bought in local markets is about -15%, versus roughly 50% for authentic product; had all samples been authentic, 99% would have yielded returns above 70%. Ashour, Gilligan, Hoel and Karachiwalla, Journal of Development Studies 2019 (IFPRI, Uganda glyphosate): the average bottle is missing 15% of active ingredient, 31% of samples contain less than 75% of the advertised concentration, farmers believe 41% of herbicide is fake, yet the price gap between a fully authentic and a zero-glyphosate bottle is only 5.8% of the average price, so the market does not punish fakes. About 60% of low-quality herbicide is attributed to agro-dealers and informal sellers removing or reusing labels. Market-wide estimates of 15-30% counterfeit inputs in East Africa circulate but lack a verified primary source, so continental scale is lower-confidence than the Ugandan measurements.

**Why solutions fail.** Anti-counterfeit tools protect the label, not the contents. Scratch-code and SMS verification (KEPHIS seed labels, mPedigree-style codes) confirm a package left a licensed factory, but Bold et al. show quality is destroyed downstream: wholesalers bulk out fertiliser or dye grain to look like hybrid seed, and retailers repack large bags into small unbranded lots stored in the open, so there is no code to scratch. Official product registers (PCPB, KEPHIS, MAAIF, CIB&RC) exist but are PDFs or web tables a farmer cannot query at the shop counter, and they say nothing about a specific dealer or batch. Lab testing is expensive and one-off (the QJE study was a research project, not a running system), and results are never published at dealer level, so dealers face no consequences. Because yields are noisy, farmers cannot learn quality from their own harvests (Bold et al.'s calibrated learning model), so reputation alone does not discipline the market; this is why the market neither corrects nor fully collapses.

**Intervention.** An open input-quality ledger that moves accountability from the label to the dealer and the batch. Four components: (1) a registry API that ingests official registered-product lists (PCPB, KEPHIS, CIB&RC, state seed catalogues) into one queryable database so any product name, registration number, batch or dealer can be checked by web, SMS or WhatsApp text and returns registered / banned / expired / not found; (2) a dealer-level quality record built from structured, geotagged farmer reports with a cheap, standardised evidence protocol (seven-day paper-towel germination test photographed daily, lot numbers, expiry photos, price paid), so patterns at one shop become visible rather than anecdotal; (3) a sentinel-sampling workflow for regulators, NGOs and seed companies: randomised mystery purchases, chain-of-custody, partner-lab nutrient or germination results, and publication per dealer and batch, turning one-off studies like Bold et al. into a continuous public dataset; (4) a dispute channel that routes substantiated cases to the regulator and records the outcome. The novelty is publishing measured quality at the point where dilution actually happens, with an open scoring method, rather than another QR code. Useful without any AI; image models for label matching are optional.

**Business model.** Farmers never pay for lookups or reporting. Revenue from (a) seed and agrochemical companies paying for brand-protection analytics and a verified-dealer programme (they lose the most from fakes); (b) regulators and donors funding sentinel sampling and lab testing, which the platform manages at lower cost than ad-hoc studies; (c) microfinance institutions and input-credit lenders paying for dealer quality scores to decide where loans can be redeemed; (d) insurers bundling quality assurance with input-linked policies. Guardrails: no pay-to-list or pay-to-improve-score, open scoring code, farmer reports owned by farmers, and company sponsors cannot see or influence individual dealer records.

**Outcomes.** Lab-measured nutrient content and germination rates at sampled shops over successive seasons (the Bold et al. metric) in covered vs uncovered districts; share of lookups returning unregistered/banned products and its trend; repeat-offence rate per dealer after publication; regulator enforcement actions traceable to platform evidence; fertiliser and hybrid-seed adoption rates and measured yields among users; share of farmer reports resolved with a documented outcome; and the gap between price and measured quality, which should widen if the market starts to price quality.

## People cannot verify a payee, caller or offer before money moves, and victims have no clear path to assert payment rights and recover funds

**Who.** Anyone receiving unsolicited contact, but losses concentrate in: adults 60+ (FBI IC3 2024: 147,127 complaints, $4.885bn, the highest per-victim losses, ~$83k average per reporting elder victim per AARP restatement); 18-29-year-olds (FTC: more likely than older adults to report losing money, especially job and online-shopping scams); limited-English-proficiency and diaspora communities (FTC formally found Spanish speakers more likely to be victimised and less likely to report; Chinese-speaking communities hit by consulate/police impersonation, ~$164k average loss per reporting victim in IC3 PSA 190328); middle-class Indians targeted by 'digital arrest' (senior citizens filed 103,488 complaints reporting Rs 4,005 crore in 2025, ~4x the average complaint); migrant and low-income job-seekers in the Philippines, Indonesia, India, Kenya, Ethiopia, Nepal and Latin America who answer fake overseas job ads; Global South adults with the highest exposure (GASA 2025: 83% of Kenyan adults encountered a scam); and small businesses hit by invoice/BEC fraud. Supply side: OHCHR (2023) ~120,000 people in Myanmar and ~100,000 in Cambodia coerced into scam compounds via fake job ads. Harm beyond money: GASA 2025 — 69% of victims report significant stress; documented suicides among pig-butchering and sextortion victims.

**Why solutions fail.** Before the loss: (1) signals are siloed by industry — banks see payee/mule patterns, telcos see numbers, platforms see profiles, registrars see domains, regulators publish warning lists (FCA, ASIC, MAS, SEC/FINRA, SEBI, IOSCO I-SCAN) in incompatible formats — none are joined at the moment a consumer decides; (2) blocklists (Safe Browsing, Truecaller/Hiya, carrier labels) cover only URLs and phone numbers, not payees, wallets, company names, job posts or profiles, and attacker infrastructure is disposable; (3) warnings are generic and mistimed — GASA 2025: 73% of adults feel confident spotting scams yet 57% were scammed, and AI removes the cues people rely on; Confirmation of Payee exists only in a few jurisdictions. After the loss: (4) reporting is fragmented and single-agency (bank, FTC, IC3, state AG, platform, carrier, exchange), with different legal rules per rail (Reg E vs. UCC 4A), no feedback loop, 36% of victims don't know whom to tell; (5) recovery depends on time-boxed rights victims have never heard of (Reg E windows, chargeback windows, UK PSR 13-month APP claim window, India's 'golden hour' 1930/NCRP freeze). The UK shows process matters: after mandatory APP reimbursement (Oct 2024) in-scope reimbursement reached 89% of lost value, 82% of claims closed within 5 business days, and in-scope APP losses fell ~21% (~£73m/yr); the US has no equivalent (GASA: only 30% who reported to their payment provider recovered anything); (6) victims are re-victimised by 'recovery scams' because no trusted post-loss process exists; (7) bank-side tools require institutional integration and do not help once money has left.

**Intervention.** A single web service covering the two moments the market leaves empty: 'check before you pay' and 'what are my rights now'. (1) Verification: one query — domain, phone, email, crypto address, company or payee name, or pasted job/investment text — returns an explainable, evidence-linked risk report assembled by joining open signals that today sit apart: Google Safe Browsing, PhishTank/OpenPhish/URLhaus, RDAP registration age, crt.sh first-seen dates, OpenCorporates/Companies House/SEC EDGAR/FINRA BrokerCheck for entity existence and licensing, every national regulator warning list (FCA, ASIC, MAS, SEC, CSA, SEBI, CVM, IOSCO I-SCAN) normalised into one openly published, versioned schema (which does not exist today), Chainabuse/Etherscan labels, FTC Sentinel and Scamwatch aggregates, and the service's own report graph, correlated at entity level (same wallet, payee, phone or recruiter reused across reports). (2) Recovery case engine: a victim, or an authorised trusted contact/caregiver, describes one incident once (rail, dates, counterparties, evidence); a deterministic, jurisdiction-specific rules-as-code engine computes which protections apply (US Reg E vs. authorised push with no reimbursement right; UK PSR mandatory APP reimbursement and 13-month deadline; India NCRP freeze and RBI pilot), every deadline, and an ordered action list; then generates and tracks all filings from one evidence pack — bank dispute letter with regulatory citations, FTC/IC3/Action Fraud/NCRP submissions, platform, exchange and carrier reports, chargebacks, ombudsman escalation when the bank misses statutory response times — with reminders, status tracking, a 'recovery-scam shield' and a trusted-contact mode. Reports feed the verification graph, closing the loop. An embeddable widget/API lets credit unions, payment and remittance apps, marketplaces and job boards show the check at the moment of payment or application. AI is optional (parsing pasted offers or screenshots); the core is joins plus rules.

**Business model.** Free for individuals (rate-limited lookups; recovery cases always free and never a percentage of recovered funds, which is the recovery-scam model). Revenue from institutions that bear the losses or the duty of care: B2B API/widget and white-label licences for credit unions, community banks, fintechs, remittance and payment apps, marketplaces and job boards — they already buy fraud tooling, must meet Reg E/PSR response deadlines, and UK mandatory APP reimbursement gives a direct financial incentive to stop payments before they leave and to receive structured, complete claims; employer and insurer benefits (EAPs, identity-theft insurance); senior-living and elder-care networks; legal-aid and consumer-protection organisations; government/agency contracts for the structured report-routing pipeline; a consented, de-identified counterparty-intelligence feed for banks and platforms. Grants from anti-fraud coalitions (AARP, Cifas-type bodies) seed jurisdiction packs and the open regulator-warning dataset. Works without AI; an LLM optionally summarises PDFs and transcripts but every sentence must link to a record.

**Business model.** The accountability layer stays free and un-gated: public report cards, briefings, base dataset, and a free tier for journalists, libraries and civil-society monitors. Revenue from: SME bidders paying for competition-aware tender alerts and market intelligence; supreme audit institutions, procurement regulators and multilateral lenders paying for internal monitoring dashboards and API access; local governments and libraries buying transparency-as-a-service and online legal-notice publishing (an existing paid obligation as states move notices off print); syndication licences to newsrooms and broadcasters; a paid API tier for structured zoning, permit and procurement data used by real-estate, construction and legal customers; and philanthropic and multilateral grants (Press Forward, Knight, Google News Initiative, OCP, World Bank, EU, national anti-corruption programmes) that already fund one-off versions. **Outcomes.** Jurisdictions receiving at least a weekly record-backed briefing; share of briefing sentences with a resolvable source link (target 100%); corrections per 1,000 items; median days from record publication to first briefing or flag delivery; flagged processes that result in a formal inquiry, audit, bid protest or annulled award (tracked via the outcome layer); published investigations citing evidence packs and pickup by surviving local outlets; change in single-bid rate, direct-award share and median submission window for watched buyers versus unwatched peers over 12-24 months; number of distinct SMEs bidding on watched buyers' tenders; precision of flags as judged by auditor feedback; public-comment sign-ups, meeting attendance and records requests in covered vs uncovered jurisdictions; resident civic-knowledge survey scores; longer-run and harder to attribute: contested seats and turnout in local elections, and municipal borrowing spreads.

## Step-free journeys break on missing, disconnected data about accessible transit paths, elevators, sidewalks and crossings

*Id:* `step-free-journey-data` · *Merged from:* Step-free journeys break on missing data: there is no connected, live picture of accessibl

**Who.** Wheelchair and mobility-scooter users; people with walking or stamina impairments (older adults, arthritis, cardiopulmonary disease, amputation); blind and low-vision pedestrians who depend on curb ramps, tactile paving and marked crossings; secondarily parents with strollers and delivery workers. Concentrated in cities with legacy rail systems (New York, London, Paris, Boston, Chicago, Berlin) and in car-dependent US suburbs with discontinuous sidewalks. Also the paratransit-dependent population that agencies must serve under ADA and equivalent laws. Population context (training knowledge, not re-verified because who.int was blocked): WHO Disability and Health fact sheet (2023) estimates 1.3 billion people, 16% of the world, live with significant disability; WHO wheelchair guidance estimates roughly 1% of any population needs a wheelchair (~80 million people).

**Scale.** Verified primary evidence (GitHub-hosted docs read 2026-11-02; web search and most hosts were blocked): (1) GTFS spec: stop-level wheelchair_boarding is Optional and '0 or empty' means 'no accessibility information'; pathways.txt, the only way to model stairs, elevators and ramps inside a station, is Optional; a feed can be fully valid while saying nothing about accessibility. (2) GTFS-Realtime: the only accessibility primitive is a generic ACCESSIBILITY_ISSUE alert effect and a per-trip WheelchairAccessible override; no standard elevator/escalator status feed exists. (3) Project Sidewalk (Univ. of Washington) has crowdsourced 3.4M+ labels of curb ramps, obstacles and missing sidewalks across 50+ cities in 10 countries because the data does not otherwise exist; AI curb-ramp detection reached human-level accuracy only in 2025 and is used mainly to validate human labels (precision threshold 0.92). (4) AccessMap routing needs bespoke per-city pipelines; OpenSidewalks schema is at v0.3 (2026-01-27). (5) Microsoft discontinued Soundscape and open-sourced a 'not turnkey' subset. (6) Wheelmap/accessibility.cloud is place-level, not path-level. Not re-verified (training knowledge): NYC MTA ~30% of 472 stations ADA-accessible in 2024, 2022 settlement commits to 95% by 2055; London Underground ~one third step-free; US NTD paratransit trips cost agencies $40-60+ vs under $10 for fixed-route. Severity: one failed elevator strands a wheelchair user mid-journey, driving trip avoidance, missed medical appointments, isolation, employment exclusion and costly paratransit shift.

**Why solutions fail.** Google and Apple Maps offer wheelchair-accessible transit routing only in a subset of cities and inherit agency GTFS, where accessibility fields are optional and often empty; they cannot model within-station paths unless the agency publishes pathways.txt, and live elevator outages (published, if at all, in agency-specific formats) rarely trigger re-routing. OpenStreetMap sidewalk, curb-ramp and crossing coverage is sparse and inconsistent, so pedestrian routers silently assume a sidewalk exists. Wheelmap answers 'can I enter this place', not 'can I get there'. The research tools with the right data model (AccessMap, Project Sidewalk, OpenSidewalks) are per-city, grant-funded and not integrated into anything riders use daily; Soundscape's discontinuation shows even big-tech consumer apps in this niche were not sustained. Critically, no product distinguishes 'no barrier' from 'no data', so users cannot calibrate trust.

**Intervention.** An open 'step-free journey graph' and alerting service. (1) Ingest every GTFS feed catalogued in the Mobility Database, extract stops, pathways, levels and wheelchair flags, and compute a per-feed accessibility data-completeness score. (2) Normalise agency elevator/escalator outage feeds (MTA, TfL, BART, WMATA, etc.) into one schema and attach outages to graph edges. (3) Merge sidewalk, curb-ramp and crossing data from OpenSidewalks, Project Sidewalk's open GeoJSON API and OSM, each edge carrying a confidence score and source. (4) Route against a personal mobility profile (max slope, max curb height, needs elevator, can manage N stairs, needs tactile or signalised crossings) and return confidence per leg; users 'watch' saved routes and get an alert when an elevator on that route fails. (5) Publish per-city scorecards: data coverage, outage frequency and duration per station, time to repair. Novel: explicit uncertainty ('no data' vs 'accessible'), outage-aware re-routing with alerts, profile-based rather than binary routing, and agency accountability metrics from the same data. Works without AI (graph fusion plus routing); open computer-vision models such as RampNet are optional for filling sidewalk gaps.

**Business model.** Riders use it free. Revenue from transit agencies and city transport departments for the outage-normalisation service and rider alerts, accessibility reporting against ADA, Equality Act and EU obligations, and paratransit-shift analytics (each trip moved from demand-response to fixed route saves the agency tens of dollars); data licensing to mapping, real-estate and mobility companies; sponsored city onboarding by disability organisations. The core graph is open-licensed so the public value does not depend on the company surviving.

**Outcomes.** Share of planned trips completed without a fallback (self-reported and inferred); 'stranded by outage' incidents per 1,000 trips; paratransit trips per registered rider before and after adoption; median minutes from elevator failure to user alert; data-completeness percentage per city over time; agency outage duration trends; retention among wheelchair-using riders.

Existing DIY tools are single-jurisdiction and passive: Suffolk LIT Lab's Massachusetts docassemble interviews (motion to stay, petition to seal), JustFix (NYC), Rentervention (Illinois), TexasLawHelp answer forms. None is triggered by the court docket itself, so a tenant who does not know a case was filed, or does not know the answer deadline (filing-to-judgment windows run 0 to 23 days by state), never reaches the form. Record sealing requires a petition most tenants never learn about, and screening companies scrape dockets before sealing happens, so the remedy arrives too late. Court e-filing systems (Tyler Odyssey) are closed, and unauthorized-practice-of-law rules keep most tools at static information.

**Intervention.** A docket-triggered eviction defense and record-relief engine. (1) Ingest public eviction dockets for a jurisdiction (the court feeds the Eviction Lab already tracks in 48 sites; open data in NYC, Philadelphia, Harris County, Maricopa); for a tenant who registers or is matched by an advocate, detect the filing, compute the jurisdiction-specific answer deadline and hearing date, and push SMS/email reminders. (2) Run a structured defense-discovery interview mapped to that jurisdiction's statutory defenses, with automated evidence checks against public datasets: CARES Act 30-day-notice coverage via HUD multifamily, LIHTC, FHA and GSE property databases; city code-violation and inspection open data; ownership and rent-registration records; and the landlord's filing concentration (the top 100 buildings account for up to 58% of filings in some cities). (3) Generate a court-ready answer with affirmative defenses, exhibit packet and fee-waiver form in the court's own format. (4) After judgment or dismissal, auto-generate the state-specific sealing/expungement petition and FCRA dispute letters to screening companies from a maintained rules table. (5) Offer an intake API handing legal aid and right-to-counsel providers a pre-built case file. Novel elements: docket-triggered timing before the deadline, and a cross-jurisdiction rules graph coupled to public-data evidence checks; it works without AI, with optional language-model help only for drafting narrative statements.

**Business model.** Never landlord-funded. Revenue from institutions that already pay to reduce defaults and attorney time: court self-help centers and eviction-diversion programs (per-case or annual license; many ARPA- or state-funded), right-to-counsel and legal aid providers (intake-triage SaaS, fundable through LSC Technology Initiative Grants), emergency rental assistance administrators, and municipal housing departments. Individual tenants always free; an optional paid record-sealing and screening-dispute service for tenants who can afford it (Upsolve-style freemium) is defensible as long as the free path is complete. Honest assessment: moderate sustainability; revenue depends on public and philanthropic budgets, but the institutional buyers are real and the cost-benefit evidence (Stout 2018) is already accepted by them.

**Outcomes.** Share of filings with an answer or appearance filed among enrolled tenants versus the jurisdiction baseline (e.g., 54% unanswered in New York); default-judgment rate; share of cases dismissed, settled with time, or won; days from filing to first contact; sealing petitions filed and granted; FCRA disputes resolved; attorney intake minutes per case for partner legal aid offices; six-month housing retention of enrolled tenants versus matched non-users.
