# Food Safety Intelligence: evidence file

Research for the question of whether Batchlight should expand into food safety intelligence, starting in India. Gathered November 2026 by six research agents using web search, then checked by an adversarial fact-checker. Some primary sites (fssai.gov.in, who.int, ec.europa.eu, sgs.com) could not be fetched from the research environment, so several primary documents were read only through search excerpts.

**Evidence grades.** *Verified*: a primary source (regulator, peer-reviewed paper, official statistics, company filing). *Reported*: credible secondary reporting. *Estimate*: a market-research or analyst figure, often unreliable. *Assumption*: an inference by the researcher, with no source.

## Corrections after the November 2026 India-readiness audit

Some rows below were re-checked against primary sources in November 2026, and the site's text was changed to match. Where a row below disagrees with this list, this list is the current position.

- **MDH and Everest samples in India.** The "28 lab reports" figure came from unnamed sources in news reports. The Health Ministry told the Lok Sabha (unstarred question 837, 26 Jul 2024) that FSSAI lifted 34 samples from MDH (25) and Everest (9) manufacturing facilities and found no traces of ethylene oxide in them. [source](https://sansad.in/getFile/lsapps/loksabhaquestions/annex/182/AU837_QkaCSZ.pdf?source=lsapps)
- **Hong Kong, April 2024.** The Centre for Food Safety's own press release covers samples of three MDH spice mixes and Everest Fish Curry Masala bought from three shops in Tsim Sha Tsui under routine surveillance. It covers only those packs sold in Hong Kong and gives no levels; all the date marks it names have passed. Singapore's recall of Everest Fish Curry Masala is known here only from news reports. [source](https://www.cfs.gov.hk/english/press/20240405_10919.html)
- **Everest cumin powder, October 2026.** Business Today and Scroll report that a food analyst found the residues and a referral food laboratory then also declared the sample unsafe. Neither says FSSAI stated that the finding applies only to that batch, so that wording was removed.
- **Patanjali red chilli powder, January 2025.** Whether FSSAI issued a public alert could not be settled (ThePrint could not be re-read consistently); we can say only that we found no public notice from FSSAI. Business Standard (PTI) prints the batch as AJD2400012 and ThePrint as JD2400012.
- **FSSAI enforcement totals.** Lok Sabha unstarred question 1092 (24 Jul 2026), Annexures III and I, gives 170,513 samples analysed and 33,808 non-conforming in 2023-24, and 223,808 analysed and 40,023 non-conforming in 2025-26 (provisional), with no split into unsafe, sub-standard and labelling failures. The same reply says 305 Food Safety on Wheels units are deployed across 35 States/UTs. It replaces the LatestLY/ANI and legalitysimplified.com links. The Rajya Sabha link on rsdebate.nic.in now redirects to a generic page, so the 6,782 unsafe / 22,603 sub-standard / 4,423 labelling split is not used on the site. [source](https://sansad.in/getFile/lsapps/loksabhaquestions/annex/188/AU1092_7TaOa5.pdf)
- **RAFT kits.** PIB's release of 31 Jul 2026 (a Lok Sabha written reply) says 88 RAFT kits have been approved and that they reduce screening time in the field. It does not repeat the "screening and surveillance only" wording, which comes from FSSAI's status list and was not re-read. [source](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2292341)
- **DART booklet.** Its disclaimer says its main aim is to create awareness in consumers about detecting food adulteration. No published data on the accuracy of its tests was found. [source](https://www.fssai.gov.in/docs/knowledge-hub/books/DART-Book.pdf)
- **Milk survey 2018.** The final report was read in full on www.fssai.gov.in; the mail.fssai.gov.in copy is no longer cited. [source](https://www.fssai.gov.in/upload/uploadfiles/files/Report_Milk_Survey_NMQS_Final_18_10_2019.pdf)
- **Edible oil survey 2020.** The report was read in full. Its 572 (12.82%) misbranded samples include oils that did not meet the required vitamin A and D fortification and oils that did not meet other labelling requirements, not fortification alone. [source](https://www.fssai.gov.in/upload/uploadfiles/files/Report_Edible_Oil_Survey_27_01_2022.pdf)
- **Turmeric (Forsyth et al. 2024).** Stanford's abstract page confirms 356 samples from 23 cities, 14% above 2 µg/g and Patna above 1,000 µg/g. The near 1:1 lead-to-chromium ratio is stated for samples with 18 µg/g of lead or more. The bvsalud.org copy is no longer cited. [source](https://fsi.stanford.edu/node/36768)
- **Honey.** The Foods 2025 study was read in full: 94% of 98 authentic samples met three of FSSAI's isotope criteria, 86% met the foreign oligosaccharide limit, and 47% did not meet the Δδ13Cmax criterion. The UK FSA honey NMR page now returns "archived" (HTTP 410) and its web-archive copy could not be opened, so it is no longer cited. The Down To Earth article of 4 Dec 2020 is about CSE's meeting with FSSAI, not the brand results; Business Today (December 2020) reports that only 3 of 13 brands passed the NMR test done by an unnamed German laboratory. [source](https://mdpi-res.com/d_attachment/foods/foods-14-01289/article_deploy/foods-14-01289.pdf)
- **Street-food colours.** The News Minute could not be opened. Business Today reports Tamil Nadu's Rhodamine B finding in cotton candy (Feb 2024) and Karnataka's figures (Mar 2024: 15 of 25 cotton candy and more than 100 of 171 gobi manchurian samples unsafe; colours found included tartrazine, carmoisine, sunset yellow and Rhodamine B). The earlier attribution of particular colours to gobi manchurian is not supported.
- **Global burden.** WHO's estimates published on 4 Jun 2026 (about 866 million illnesses and 1.5 million deaths from unsafe food in 2021) replace the 2015 FERG figures for 2010. [source](https://www.who.int/news/item/04-06-2026-unsafe-food-causes-866-million-illnesses-and-1.5-million-deaths-annually--young-children-at-highest-risk)

## Food safety problems in India

**Researcher summary.** India's food-safety failures are real, but the dramatic headline figures need careful reading. National sampling finds about 18-20% of samples "non-conforming" (2023-24: 19.8%; FY26: 17.9%). The truly "unsafe" share is much smaller, about 4% of analysed samples in 2023-24. Most failures are sub-standard quality or labelling problems.



Most serious problems (health harm x prevalence), as I read the evidence:

(1) Microbial contamination. It carries the largest disease burden: WHO FERG estimates about 150M illnesses and 175k deaths a year in South-East Asia (reference year 2010). India measures it poorly: there are only small local studies, and official mid-day-meal outbreak counts are far below IDSP-based tallies.

(2) Chronic chemical hazards. Lead chromate in turmeric (peer-reviewed; extreme in Bihar-sourced samples), aflatoxin M1 in milk (5.7%), and pesticide residues (about 3% of samples above limits).

(3) Economic adulteration of dairy. About 37-38% of milk and dairy samples fail, mostly on quality. Analogue paneer is mislabelled, ghee and honey are adulterated, and edible oils are blended (24% failed quality tests).



Most underserved (my own inference): street-food and school-meal hygiene, and getting regulatory actions to the public. FSSAI ordered a batch-level recall of chilli powder in January 2025 without any public alert, and the rules do not require recalls to be disclosed. Enforcement capacity is thin: there are too few food safety officers, few NABL-accredited labs, and only 1,918 convictions in FY26.



Assumption: Batchlight fits the dissemination gap (recalls, non-conforming batches, alerts from export partners) better than it fits detection. The honey and ghee cases show that adulterants adapt to the tests in force, and lab results get contested. Any detection claim, AI or not, needs independent validation.

## Pesticides, contaminants and export rejections

**Researcher summary.** Indian food has real, repeated chemical and microbial problems, but most of them show up at export borders. Government monitoring finds few domestic samples over the pesticide limit (about 2.8-3.1%, from parliamentary replies). Export failures recur in specific products: ethylene oxide in sesame (2020-21) and spices (2024); pesticides in rice, where EU rejections of non-basmati rice rose from 3 to 37 between 2020 and 2024, and in tea; Salmonella in spices sent to the US; banned antibiotics in shrimp; and aflatoxin in groundnuts. The most serious health finding is a domestic adulteration problem: lead chromate in turmeric. Patna samples exceeded 1000 µg/g against an FSSAI limit of 10 µg/g.



Economic impact is poorly measured. The predicted collapse of spice exports in 2024 did not happen; FY25 exports reached a reported record of $4.72bn. No aggregate rupee figure for rejected consignments was found. The costs fall on individual exporters (rejection, destruction, US detention without examination, reputation damage) and on commodity boards, which already run mandatory testing (Spices Board) and batch traceability (APEDA groundnuts).



Detection is a laboratory problem: LC/GC-MS/MS for residues and ethylene oxide, ICP-MS for metals. I found no evidence that software or AI can replace these tests.



The alert-feed intelligence market already has established vendors: SGS Nexus (Digicomply plus FOODAKAI) and Fera HorizonScan. One important limit for Batchlight's batch-matching model is that the public RASFF Window does not name brands or operators. FDA refusal data does name firms.



My inference (an assumption, not tested): the opening most worth testing is pre-shipment MRL compliance intelligence for Indian exporters of rice, spices, tea and shrimp. It would map each commodity-pesticide pair against the limits of each destination (EU, US, Codex, FSSAI) and track refusals and import alerts against named exporters. I found no evidence that anyone would pay for this.

## Detection and prevention technologies: scientific validation

**Researcher summary.** Reliable detection still depends on confirmatory lab chemistry: LC-MS/MS, GC-MS/MS, HPLC, elemental analysis, isotope/NMR and DNA barcoding. In India, a multi-residue pesticide test costs about Rs 10,000-14,000 per sample and takes 7-10 days. Rapid tools are legitimate screens: FSSAI RAFT kits (88 approved by 2026, for screening and surveillance only), milk strips, antibiotic and aflatoxin M1 lateral-flow tests, and enzyme (AChE) pesticide tests. But independent sensitivity/specificity data are sparse, and positives need lab confirmation. FSSAI's own 2018 survey of 6,432 milk samples found aflatoxin M1 above the limit in 5.7% and antibiotics in 1.2%. Safety-relevant adulterants turned up in only 12 samples. Honey shows the limits of single methods. Isotope tests miss C3 syrups such as rice syrup and fail about 6% of authentic Indian honey. NMR depends on a reference database that FSSAI says India lacks.



AI/ML: on spectra it can classify known adulterants well for one matrix and one device. On current evidence it is not reliable in the field. A 2026 systematic review of 83 records found none with inter-laboratory validation and none documenting routine-monitoring use. Calibration transfer between devices, low-level adulteration and adulterants missing from training data remain unsolved. I found no validation evidence for camera or phone-only 'AI adulteration detection'. Vendor accuracy claims (Raman-1, strip makers) are unverified.



Prevention: traceability (GS1, blockchain) speeds up record retrieval (Walmart: 7 days down to 2.2 s), but it cannot verify the physical product. GFSI requires food-fraud vulnerability assessments and supplier controls.



Assumption: the defensible move for Batchlight is to aggregate validated results into batch-level intelligence. Inputs would be regulator alerts (such as the 2024 Hong Kong/Singapore ethylene oxide spice findings), RAFT approvals and lab reports. It should not build a detector or claim AI detection. ## Existing solutions and competitors

**Researcher summary.** India's food safety market is crowded where the money is: testing labs, rapid kits, commodity quality-grading and free government tools. FSSAI already runs FoSCoS licence checks, the Food Safety Connect complaints app, the FIRA portal for rejected imports, and since 18 Mar 2026 a FoSCoS Food Recall module that gives each recall a unique ID. Global TIC labs (testing, inspection and certification: Eurofins, Mérieux, Intertek, TÜV) dominate testing. Rapid kits go through FSSAI's RAFT approval scheme (88 approved as of Apr 2026), and FSSAI itself limits them to screening only. Funded Indian startups (AgNext $21M in 2021, Intello $8.3M in total, TraceX about $1M) sell B2B quality grading or farm traceability. None of them sells recall or alert intelligence. Global intelligence platforms (FoodChain ID, SGS Food Nexus, which merged Digicomply and Agroknow in 2026, and Fera HorizonScan) aggregate recalls and fraud incidents for multinationals on quote-only enterprise pricing. I could not confirm that any of them ingests FSSAI data. US traceability tools (Trustwell, iFoodDS, ReposiTrak) are driven by FSMA 204 (the US FDA food traceability rule) and show no India presence. India has no equivalent mandate, so paid traceability demand there is unproven.



The likely white space (my assumption, not verified) is narrow and fits Batchlight. Nobody verified links FSSAI recall and FIRA records at batch level to the businesses downstream that hold the stock: retailers, quick-commerce stores, distributors, hospital and institutional kitchens. Nobody tracks recall closure either, and a consultancy found about 89% of FoSCoS recalls without documented closure. The closest incumbent is GS1 India DataKart Trace, which claims "targeted recall as per FSSAI notification". Its adoption is unknown and it must be checked.



There is a big caution. India's recall volume is low: about 154 records in roughly 2.5 years, per one analysis. A product that only matches India recalls may have too few events to sell on its own. Detection-hardware claims, such as AI or e-nose adulteration detection, mostly rest on what companies say about themselves, not on independent validation.

## Customers, market size and investment

**Researcher summary.** Market-size figures are weak. Estimates of India's food-testing market differ about 16-fold between firms (IMARC USD 906M, TechSci USD 55M). Global testing, about USD 22–25B in 2024, is mostly labs and instruments. Compliance software is about USD 1–2B (Prof Research). ReposiTrak, a listed food traceability network that calls itself the world's largest, earned only USD 22.6M in FY2025.



The firm evidence of demand comes from regulators. About 20% of FSSAI samples failed standards in 2023-24. The EU has raised border checks on Indian okra, cumin, cloves and vanilla. The Spices Board has required ethylene oxide (EtO) testing since the 2024 Hong Kong and Singapore recalls. One older report puts the cost of a rejected rice container at Rs 15 lakh. FSSAI has a 30% or 45-day shelf-life rule for food sold online, and licences have been suspended at Zepto, Blinkit and Instamart sites.



Customer groups (my own assumptions, not sourced):

- Exporters (spices, rice, seafood): most urgent, already pay labs, the owner or export-QA head signs. Best fit for matching EU (RASFF), US FDA and FSSAI import-rejection (FIRA) alerts to product, origin and lot.

- Quick commerce: urgent, large budgets, but likely to build in-house. The compliance or operations head signs.

- FMCG brands: medium urgency, legally required to keep a recall plan. The QA head signs.

- QSR chains: medium urgency, driven by audits. Global software prices are about USD 100–300 per site per month; India is probably lower.

- State food-safety departments: small, irregular budgets spent mostly on lab equipment.

- Hospitals, caterers and consumers: no evidence found that they would pay.



Investment: Indian food-quality startups raised modest sums (AgNext USD 21M Series A, Intello USD 5.9M, TraceX about USD 1M). Globally, money goes to workflow software (SafetyCulture valued at about USD 1.7B), while one tracker lists the handheld detector Inspecto as shut down.



Recommendation from this track: build export-compliance alert intelligence as a Batchlight extension, not detection hardware.

## Data availability for a food alert product

**Researcher summary.** Global food-alert data is mostly machine-readable today. India's is not. Batchlight's existing ingest-and-match pattern works directly on: openFDA food enforcement (RES, with a free-text code_info field for lots), the UK FSA Food Alerts API (JSON/CSV, OGL v3, keyless, batchDescription in productDetails), Canada's combined Recalls and Safety Alerts JSON/CSV (CFIA food records mixed in), the Hong Kong CFS XML feed, the RASFF Window open-data API (2020 onward, public summaries only) and FSANZ/SFA RSS. FDA import refusals need a free authorisation key. FDA import alerts are firm-level HTML Red Lists.



India: FSSAI has no structured recall feed, no brand or batch-level unsafe-sample registry and no official licence-verification API that I could find. The 2017 Recall Regulation only says FSSAI "may" set up a recall portal. Recalls are mainly the food business's own duty, through press releases and ads. In Jan 2025 FSSAI ordered a batch recall (Patanjali) without any public alert. In 2026 FSSAI began announcing batch-level recalls and named brands through X posts and press releases (Wonderland Raisins in July, Everest cumin batch E080668761 in Oct). Batch numbers exist, but only as unstructured text that has to be curated by hand. Surveillance data is aggregate only (state-wise counts in parliamentary PDF annexures). FIRA lists rejected imports through a web portal. States publish mostly hygiene raids on X. FoSCoS can be checked by hand (captcha), and commercial KYC vendors resell verification APIs.



Conclusion (assumption): an India food-alert product today would rely on manual or LLM-assisted curation of a small, irregular stream of FSSAI posts. Coverage would be low, and with few India recalls carrying batch numbers, there is not enough data to match against stock lists. Export-side India signals (FDA refusals and alerts, RASFF India-origin) are machine-readable now. WebFetch was blocked for open.fda.gov and fssai.gov.in, so I could not read those primary pages and relied on search results.
